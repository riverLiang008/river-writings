# Living Below the Trees

2025.09.24

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

My time seems to have fallen into a kind of stasis. Four months ago, I spent just eight days in the Himalayas in Nepal, yet those eight days yielded something infinite—an infinity of experiences, sensations and emotions that I can return to in memory and live through again.

But two months of working life have passed, and they seem to have been compressed into a single day: bland, punctuated by my various attempts to resist that blandness and confinement. It is not as though nothing happens at work. There is the painful memory of being criticised for my ‘student mentality’, and all the absurd ‘efforts’ I am forced to make to meet so-called ‘targets’. I seem to be beginning to feel how absurd these activities carried out in the name of creating economic value are, while the things I truly cherish produce nothing in worldly terms.

I see this reality so clearly, and in it I see my own powerlessness. I acknowledge that powerlessness.

Idealism? Idealism can barely survive in human cities, and in the end it collapses into the void. In my mind, I often banish myself to the wilderness. My soul circles there for a while, then returns to the city where my body remains. Yes, I am powerless, unable to refuse all this rot and mire. I live in the mud.

Here is a monologue I wrote at my desk some time earlier:

> ‘Sitting at my desk now, I feel miserable. I have only just completed my first month at work. The relationships I have no choice but to maintain; the difficult tasks assigned to me by chance, which are unlikely to yield much in the way of results; the scheming among colleagues—all of it exhausts me. Yet within this environment, my situation is not bad. Some people might even be said to envy it. But anyone who wants to remain themselves, to preserve a “natural” attitude towards the world, has to struggle inside that vast machine of alienation called “society”.
>
> Calvino created a “life in the trees” in his writing, yet admitted in an interview that he himself “lived below the trees”.<sup id="note-ref-1"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> I think back to my first year at university, when my unease and fear of the world were far greater than they are now. Looking back over the path I have taken, I feel as though I have been flung back to some starting point.
>
> I detest all definite purposes, carefully contrived rationality, and performances that pretend to be effortless. If I could choose, I would do things that produce no economic value at all.’

Whenever the flow of my self meets the obstructions reality places in its way, I think of those few words the elderly woman who read my fortune from my face said to me. Perhaps this is the allure of prophecy: it gives you some inexpressible hope to cling to, as though grasping at a straw in the void. She told me I was standing on the last step of all my trials; once I crossed it, the road ahead would be smooth. In fact, I have recalled those words more than once. After we parted, I did internships, studied abroad, travelled and looked for work. Everything seemed to go well. Now I even have a place where I can be quietly alone, think and create—the little home I rent. But the self always longs to expand, and I still face that most universal of human conditions: the soul yearns to rise to heaven and sink into its eternal tranquillity, while the body falls heavily to the ground, stained with the filth of the world. Life’s difficulties are like the heads of the mythical Hydra: cut one off, and new ones grow in its place.

But I am no longer the person I was in my first year at university. I no longer doubt my own worth. The new difficulty is to maintain a fragile balance within the immense tension between myself and the reality around me—to try, amid the obstructions this absurd world places in my way, to express myself more discreetly and indirectly, allowing my self to flow freely.

In an interview, Calvino was asked, ‘What would you say to someone weary of life, to save their life?’ He replied:<sup id="note-ref-2"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

> I would tell them that life is rich and varied for other people, and so it can hold much to treasure; that tomorrow will never be the same as yesterday; that there is always something waiting for you; that first you must try to step outside yourself, to take part in other people’s lives and in the things around you. Literature itself is a record of values, of ways of regarding the value of life; it can distinguish what is worth living for amid things that seem worthless and full of negativity. I would never say to someone pessimistic and weary of life, ‘Ah, life is so beautiful! Ah, I hope you will see it, that you will understand!’ No, I would say that life is full of troubles and strewn with thorns. As long as you keep going, you will always have troubles; any joyful experience you have will bring you new troubles. But that is life. Only within it will you sometimes find something worthwhile—perhaps tomorrow, on the very day when you feel most dejected. A year later, or five or ten years later, you will still remember it, and feel that what it gave you was truly extraordinary; and what it gave you came through suffering, through worry, through all these things. That is what I would tell them, without trying to sing them “La Vie en rose”. Life is not a bed of roses, but that is precisely what makes it our element; it is why someone who encounters many problems can be closer to life and enjoy it more than someone who is wholly indifferent or for whom everything goes smoothly. I connect this question with the question of bitterness: some of you have tasted bitterness in my work and asked me why it is so bitter. Because that is the taste of life.

Let us hope so. I hope he is right.
