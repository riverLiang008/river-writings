# I Once Had Great Prospects

2025.11.08

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

I once had great prospects. Now I am just someone small, with a dim future. I so desperately hope that the person I used to be has not faded within me. I keep wanting to make sure I have not lost myself.

8 November 2025. A rare weekend to myself. Everything around me has finally grown quiet, and I am surrounded by myself. My past self was so bright, so dazzling, that she has almost become a secret worry deep within me. I stand on the ruins of my collapsed past and look around, but I do not know where to go next. I am still young. Perhaps there are still endless possibilities. I cannot bear to watch those possibilities slowly contract, dwindling at last to a barely perceptible crack, while my youth slips away—and with it my energy, my passion, my desires, my dreams. I do not want that. I am still young, but compared with who I was a few months ago, I am no longer quite so young.

Watching the people I once knew grow dispirited and indistinct, one by one, leaves me with a bitter feeling. Our generation has barely begun to live, yet already we must learn to survive among the ruins of history, trying, in that struggle, to find a little meaning, a little light, a harbour where the heart can rest. But wave after wave sweeps us up again, back into turmoil that is uncertain, unclear, bewildering. I feel as though I live in an underground cage. I strike a match, light myself with its tiny flame, reach up to touch the cracks in the low ceiling and gauge my chances of escape—but no one knows whether a larger cage lies beyond this dungeon. No one knows what it looks like outside. I have only one thought: get out, get out, get out.

What really kills you here is the slow corrosion of time—it eats away at youth, vitality and enthusiasm, until a person becomes a puddle of mud—indistinct, shapeless. Get out? Get out? Get out?

I think of the little girl in the 2010 film *True Grit*. After that magnificent, sweeping adventure, her life seems to me to settle into dullness and stagnation. Those around her see her as a “cranky old maid”. She has lost an arm. Seen from behind, she is solitary and steadfast, like a tree in the wind.

I envy my past self—untouched by life's complications, innocent and brave, always keeping a certain distance from the world—standing on the “balcony of thought”, meeting the world's uncertainty with youthful brightness, self-examination and expression that strains a little too hard. Trying to understand the world by “seeking truth”: analysing, conceptualising, searching for logic. A time of passionate faith in reason—the world had not yet pressed all the way in, and the boundary between self and world was still clear.

Now I dream of lightness and freedom, of one day crossing this sea of silence and despair to reach the other shore. Though I do not know what that shore should look like. I only know that my journey into the wider world should not be broken off by force. Yet here I am, mired in mud.

At the beginning of my working life, a little over three months ago, I wrote an essay called “Julian”. I am afraid its metaphor will become a self-fulfilling prophecy, as Tang Bohu's poem of melancholy seems to have become for him. Sustained by the humane radiance of the Greek and Roman gods, Julian endured the long confinement of his youth, then ascended the imperial throne amid fate's rewards and punishments. Yet his resistance was so weak, so small, so brief and vulnerable in the current of history. Julian fell on campaign in his early thirties. An age gradually passed away. As one of its last resisters, Julian was consigned to a dusty page of history. To me then, it felt like a death of Romanticism.

But the first thing that matters is to get out.
