# January Fourteenth

2026.01.17

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

14 January was a special day. That was when I received my first interview invitation—as though a crack had opened in a room sealed tight, letting sunlight and air rush in together. I drank them in greedily, yet feared losing them. Joy was mixed with unease and apprehension.

These past six months have been the period when I have most often turned to “fortune-telling”. In desperation, we cannot help pinning our hopes on things that seem to “float”, then clinging to them.

This makes me think of the philosopher-emperor Marcus Aurelius.

In his *Meditations*, he writes that the soul should be ready to leave the body when necessary, but that this readiness should arise from one's own judgment, with thought and dignity, rather than what he criticises as Christian obstinacy.<sup id="note-ref-1"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

Yes. This is a “great” ideal. Yet living up to it requires extraordinary strength of mind. How many ordinary people living through turmoil can truly do so? Marcus himself was initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries.<sup id="note-ref-2"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> I cannot help wondering whether even he needed something beyond reason to lean on.

Sometimes I cannot help asking whether the “thought” I have always cherished, and sometimes secretly taken pride in, is merely a talent for performance. Like a violinist standing on stage, playing for an audience. Whether they understand or not, they applaud in the end. That is why I so deeply admire Wang Yangming: he proposed the “unity of knowledge and action”, and truly lived by it throughout his life. At the end, he said, “This heart is luminous; what more is there to say?” How stirring.

In my character, vulnerability and toughness coexist, each a quality of the other—“tough vulnerability”, “vulnerable toughness”. I hope to come closer to the latter.

On 16 January, I clashed with a manager at work—I insisted on not working over the weekend—I needed to prepare for an interview at another company (which, of course, had to remain an absolute secret). The self and the world had finally collided, and I chose myself. I offered to stay late on Friday and finish the task—but that clashed with a team dinner. So my manager's manager stepped in, told me to attend the dinner, and said I would not have to work over the weekend.

Writing about Aurelius, the historian Cassius Dio observes that he did not enjoy the good fortune he deserved: he was physically frail and beset by difficulties throughout almost his entire reign. Yet Dio admires him all the more for this—amid extraordinary difficulties, he not only endured himself but also preserved the empire.<sup id="note-ref-3"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup>

This was the “context” of Marcus's life. Whether on a grand scale or a small one, human beings in adversity present a similar picture. “Persist in doing what you believe is right, even if others suffer as a result”: in this respect, Hadrian<sup id="note-ref-4"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> succeeded—he was entirely at ease with his own reasoning and saw it as only natural. Marcus was different. He had an unusually strong moral awareness—a person like that might succeed as a statesman, but at the cost of immense pain.

These are the histories and people I read about as a student that left a deep impression on me. Now I piece them together into a context of my own. I carry things with me as I walk, things I am destined never to put down. That is why my steps are heavy.

And this is the first time I have faced a problem so “vast”, because I am about to set out—or have already set out—on a road with no “answer”.

The best ending would be—to die fighting a bear, like the protagonist of *Legends of the Fall*—because his was a fitting death.<sup id="note-ref-5"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup><sup id="note-ref-6"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>
