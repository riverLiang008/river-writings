# Between Two Worlds

2026.01.24

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

Living at the boundary between two worlds, with the old life not yet entirely behind me, I am afraid that one moment's inattention will make me miss the new one.

At noon on 22 January, the integration work<sup id="note-ref-1"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> was postponed until 7:30 that evening. A preceding stage had already been held up for four hours, and the staff involved were still trying to locate the problem. At two that afternoon, the expected start time moved to 1:30 a.m. on 23 January. I began to grow uneasy at my desk.

At five, it was pushed back to 5:30 a.m. on 23 January. The work was expected to take two hours, finishing at 7:30.

But I already had a second-round interview at another company scheduled for nine that morning.

“Please, no more delays… Please, no more…”

In my experience, integration work was always pushed back, never brought forward. There was nothing I could do but pray to God.

At 5:30 p.m., riding my electric scooter home, I felt the wind against my face and silently recited: “Avalokiteshvara Bodhisattva, practising the profound perfection of wisdom, saw that all five aggregates were empty…”<sup id="note-ref-2"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

At 6:30, I got home and lay down to rest before the night's work. On the sofa, I forced myself to sleep. Never before had I needed to count time minute by minute like this. I could no longer imagine myself on the afternoon of 23 January: would I have made it through, or fallen back into that darkness? The future had not yet collapsed. All I could do was force myself to sleep in the present.

I woke at eight that evening and checked the work group chat. The start had been brought forward to 1:30 a.m. Again I prayed to God. I ordered coconut water and fresh chicken and made myself coconut chicken soup. Ate, showered. Went back to sleep.

At 10:30, an urgent phone call. A colleague told me the work had been brought forward to 11:30 p.m. I rode my electric scooter to the office through the cold night wind. Roadside stalls were still open, and people were out having late-night snacks. I felt a little calmer. But the dust had not settled; things could still change.

At 11:10, I arrived at the office, only to be told there was another fault on site. Another delay. I unfolded the camp bed by my desk and lay down for a rest. In the darkness, I heard my own breathing. Out, in, out, in…

Every so often, I checked the group chat on my phone. No news.

Close to one in the morning, I heard someone call my name. At last! I let out a breath. Please let it all go smoothly.

At 1:30 a.m., I entered the cleanroom. I began operating the software, reporting each step over an online call. I felt calmer now. At this point, it was unlikely to be pushed back until morning.

At 3:45 a.m., I came out of the cleanroom. I got on the electric scooter parked outside the office and rode home. I took a photo and sent it to my family's WeChat group. The biting air filled my lungs. But inside, I felt bright and clear. A new world was about to come rushing in. I had given it everything and, with God's help, caught hold of it. The roadside stalls were all gone now, and there was hardly anyone on the streets. Empty, cold, desolate.

At 4:10, I got home and went back to sleep.

At 7:35, I left for the interview, using the time off I had earned from the night's integration work. This spared me having to ask my immediate supervisor for leave.

8:50. Outside the company's office. A deep breath. First, I escaped into the toilets nearby.

I told myself: “Don't be afraid. You've already ‘braved’ Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia on your own.<sup id="note-ref-3"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> You even took Mum to Annapurna Base Camp in Nepal, 4,100 metres above sea level. So don't be afraid.”

During the interview, there were questions I didn't actually know how to answer. “Although… what I do know is…” I said, steering the conversation elsewhere. I stayed calm, stayed relaxed. An hour passed, and we had already gone beyond the allotted time. They gave me an extra programming problem and asked me to explain how I would approach it.

I asked if I could have some time. They agreed. Half an hour later, I knew I was ready. I explained my approach to one of the interviewers. He listened closely. From his expression, I guessed I had passed.

The interview ended at 10:30. But by the time I got home, it was already 12:50 p.m. I dozed on the sofa for ten minutes, then headed back to work.

No one knew that making it from 22 January to 23 January meant as much to me as the day I boarded a plane to Malaysia for my first solo trip abroad.

I had survived a battle for my life.

No one would have imagined that the integration work had unexpectedly earned me enough time off to attend the interview, even if it was only half a day. My colleagues and supervisor probably never dreamed that I had used those few hours to interview at another company.

At five on the afternoon of 23 January, HR called to tell me I had passed the interview.

Afterwards, I had a long phone call with Mum. Slept a long sleep. Time moved on to 24 January.

Yes. I am still living at the boundary between two worlds, with the old life not yet entirely behind me. I am afraid that one moment's inattention will make me miss the new one. The future is still floating in the void.

Does time like this hold meaning? I have survived a battle for my life, but the storm is not quite over. I live in that temporary calm. I cannot forget these two days. In those helpless moments when all I could do was toss the coin, I learned to pray, to keep praying. I learned a way of living—before the future collapses, try to rest in the present—to create meaning here and now.<sup id="note-ref-4"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>
