# A Sleepwalker's Account

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

*For the treatment of quotations from John Williams's Augustus, see the [translator's note](#translation-note).*

**Preface:** This essay abandons the pattern of posing a question and attempting an answer. It turns instead to thought, emotion, consciousness, weaving language upon language—for if we are truly to answer the questions the world puts to us, no amount of questioning, logic, or theory will ever be enough… Its approach is something like that of Antonio Porchia's *Voices*, or Laozi's *Dao De Jing*: to remain in a state of chaos, at one with the world itself.

Sometimes I confuse the strength of my will with the strength that my fate lends it. Sometimes I am more inclined to believe that my will adds fuel to the fire of my life. But lately, while reading *Roman History*, what I have felt more strongly is the will that fate imposes on an individual. An age and its people together make the fate of that age and those people—the soul stands by, lucid, watching the flesh and its desires sink, watching the age become a lump of mud, an absurd, grotesque mass of chaos. Even those periods that later historians call “golden ages” are no exception.

I am impatient to understand myself, hoping to draw strength from that understanding. Yet I am in no hurry either. After all, I seem to have “plenty” of time. I still have a long life to get through.

I was precocious. That quickness, that early understanding, is a source of strength for me; it is also a source of suffering. As I gradually entered adulthood, I seemed to find some ease and balance. But this was no performance I was “contributing” to the world and to myself. My “ease” comes, and can only come, from my having let go of my resistance to experiencing loneliness and pain. I accepted my pain, and in doing so I accepted myself.

But I am also slow. My slowness often keeps me from sensing other people's troubles and pain. I am surprised that they should have this worry or that, surprised by their “quickness”—how quickly they seize everything society gives them, then take it all for “reality.” I wonder at my own slowness, yet I have to keep up, following in their footsteps. I have not chosen exile from the world entirely. I am an incomplete poet, an incomplete man of the world. I am worldly, but not altogether worldly; I long to rise above it, yet still choose to live beneath the trees.<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-1" href="#note-1" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 1">[1]</a></sup> So when I look closely at this “I,” I too find something strange: a life on both sides of a contradiction. Or perhaps—I am the contradiction itself. My strangeness makes my harmony.

On one side I affirm—love, beauty, goodness, wholeness; on the other I deny—forgetting, ugliness, evil, brokenness. On one side I become a worldly person of “ability,” pursuing impressive credentials, work, and experiences, desiring wealth, power, reputation—apparently fulfilling what “they” expect of me. On the other I exile myself. There is a Siberian forest in my heart. I live alone in an imagined, uninhabited cabin; my breath turns a few times in the air before merging with the cold. Do I enjoy it? I do, and I torment myself. Yet I have not become someone with depression. That is a miracle. What has spared me the spiritual exile of depression? Is it my love? My love comes from my indifference, and my indifference from my passion. I often find it strange that I live among other people at all.

Do I often look down from above and scrutinize “them”? I have no standing to scrutinize anyone. Yet a glimpse of some corner of “their” inner lives often astonishes me. I remember a photograph: an Indian ascetic had driven an extremely long iron nail into his hand. The metal passed through his palm; over the years, flesh and iron had grown together. It was a chilling sight. People seem to be like this. They drive a nail into some part of the soul, then live with that foreign object for years.

I pass gravestone after gravestone of those who have fallen in spirit—can I call them fallen? Can I bring them flowers? Does opening my eyes mean I am awake?

Do we find it strange when we look in the mirror? Are we not strange ourselves? Humans really are the most misshapen, absurd, ridiculous creatures in this world. I am one of them; I am no exception. But sometimes I laugh at my own absurdity: like a suddenly abandoned child of God, fallen from the divine realm into this ludicrous condition of being “human,” yet still obliged to play my part “seriously.” The “I” has been forcibly wedged into its role. The thought makes me laugh again.

Whatever else may be true, my dream, my ultimate hope for myself, has remained the same since an afternoon in 2022—or perhaps it was 2023. I want my life to embody the dignity of being human. Perhaps it was the call of that dignity that Zeno answered when he met death calmly in *The Abyss* (《苦炼》). A poignant heroism?

If my extremely limited experience of life has taught me anything so far, it is weariness—endless weariness. However much enthusiasm I show for life, it cannot conceal the weariness deepest within me.

Though I have yet to set foot in most of the world—far short of my ambition to conquer it—my travels over the past year seem to have slowly lifted the veil from a world that was once so mysterious to me. One day I woke and found that life was much the same everywhere.

As I once wrote: “Over this year, I went to many places—many, that is, compared with my earlier life—across a wide stretch of the world. I met all kinds of people, of different backgrounds, nationalities, ages. I learned something important: whatever I might think of them, I would not judge them by my own values. I also came to understand that however different people appear, they are so alike; and that—however alike people are—they are very different. These experiences nourished me deeply. I can say that I ‘understand’ people better now; I am also more myself.” Am I a sleepwalker? In a sense, yes.

I am a nostalgic person. When I come across a book I love, I read ravenously, on rare occasions even late into the night.

I take an interest in a history and a civilization with almost nothing to do with me. For no particular reason, I grow fond of some historical figure, imagine his life, feel what he once felt, pretend he was me in another lifetime. This is a game of reincarnation I have made for myself. Since I began to sense that consciousness exists beyond linear time, I have become absorbed in experiencing those fragments of the past that happen to enter my awareness.

I spend most of my time alone. This has greatly nourished my imagination of the world, making me king of a kingdom called the self.

Yet although solitude has nourished the self, I also understand—perhaps the Buddha is right—that happiness and peace, even bliss, seem to come from relinquishing the self. Could this also be where the meaning of human lovemaking lies?

> “…Some give their whole lives to this momentary pleasure, and when the body enters its inevitable decline, they become resentful and empty. They are resentful and empty because they have known only the pleasure, not its meaning. For, contrary to what we might suppose, erotic love is the least selfish of all kinds of love; it seeks union with another, and so an escape from the self. Of course this love dies earliest, declining with the body that bears it. No doubt for this reason many consider it the lowest kind of love. Yet it is precisely because it will perish, and because we know it will perish, that it is all the more precious; and once we have known it, we are no longer irretrievably trapped and exiled within ourselves.” (*Augustus*; provisional back-translation.)

For “…I have gradually come to believe that sooner or later there is a moment in every person's life when he knows—whatever else he may understand, whether or not he can put what he knows into words—that terrible fact: he is alone, separate; he can be nothing other than his poor self.” (*Augustus*; provisional back-translation.)

But “…although union with another intoxicates us with its sensual pleasure, there is a love more powerful and enduring than this; and this love is also more powerful and enduring than the Platonic love through which we become ourselves by contemplating the secrets of another… The scholar's love for his texts, the philosopher's for his ideas, the poet's for his words: these are all such love. Thus Ovid is not alone in his northern exile at Tomis, nor are you alone in choosing to devote the rest of your life to your work in distant Damascus. So pure a love needs no living object; and so it is acknowledged as the highest kind of love, for its object approaches the absolute.” (*Augustus*; provisional back-translation.)

Separation has made our predicament. At every moment we long to become one with all beings—which is to say, with the other.

The world is an immense swamp. I struggle forward on my belly, at times composed, at times frantic. Yet whatever happens, in the midst of that enormous question, I have not turned away.

However many times I toss a coin or draw a tarot card, I must keep on “searching”—like a sculpture of a thinker.

> “As a man grows older, as the world becomes less and less his concern, he thinks more and more about the forces that drive him through time. The gods are surely indifferent to this pitiful creature struggling toward his fate; they speak to him so obliquely that in the end he must decide for himself what their signs mean.”<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-2" href="#note-2" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 2">[2]</a></sup>

The past is disappearing—as memory grows dim, it becomes chaos. The present is disappearing too, as time passes, second by second. I am disappearing. Existence is always, and can only be, a predicament.

### Translator's note on quotations

The four passages attributed to John Williams's *Augustus* have been translated back into English by ChatGPT from the quotations in the author's Chinese essay. They are not transcriptions of Williams's original English. The wording and omissions have not been verified against an English edition; the Chinese edition used by the author has not yet been identified. The passages are retained here as they appear in the Chinese essay, pending verification.
