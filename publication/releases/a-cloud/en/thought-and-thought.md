# How Thought Murders Thought

<p class="chapter-subtitle">Attachment to the Self and Modern Evil: Reading the Vimalakīrti Sūtra<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-1" href="#note-1" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 1">[1]</a></sup></p>

<em>Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.</em>

<em>Quotations in this translation have been translated from the Chinese essay. They are not quotations taken from published English translations or, in Pankaj Mishra's case, verified against his English original. See the [translator's note](#translation-note).</em>

When I wrote the first draft, I had been acquainted with Buddhism for barely four months. I could not claim to have learned much, or understood much through experience. The supposedly “profound” insights I had into the world often turned out to have echoes in texts from the human past. Reading brought moments of understanding, but they did not necessarily connect with my own experience of life. I could not even be sure whether these thoughts were bringing me closer to reality or taking me further away.

This uncertainty also came from my bewilderment about knowledge. Some theories that ought to help us understand reality have become an ironic commentary on it; forces that ought to oppose inhumanity have become its accomplices—thought has murdered thought, language has murdered language. Civilisation seems to stand upon exquisite ruins. For me, the word “civilisation” has gradually lost its inherent promise of something good.

All this may seem beside the point. Yet if we ask why we learn, perhaps these apparent digressions are what matter most. We place disciplines of knowledge on pedestals, then dissect them as we might dissect a corpse, deploying all manner of quantitative methods and producing meticulous reports. Yet knowledge can remain outside us all the while: we study it without allowing it to touch our lives; we discuss the circumstances of others without feeling that they have anything to do with us. “Nothing to do with me”—this is the attitude I want to question here. How does it become a breeding ground for inhumanity?

Breaking attachment to the self is a recurrent theme in Buddhism, and one that appears throughout the <em>Vimalakīrti Sūtra</em>. In the chapter on skilful means, images such as “This body is like a mass of foam: it cannot be grasped; this body is like a bubble: it cannot endure…” point to the body's unreliability. Clinging to the body can be understood as one form of attachment to the self. In the chapter in which Mañjuśrī asks after Vimalakīrti's illness, Vimalakīrti answers: “From ignorance and craving, my illness arises; because all sentient beings are ill, I am ill. If the illness of all sentient beings were to cease, my illness would cease…”<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-2" href="#note-2" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 2">[2]</a></sup> Here Vimalakīrti connects his own illness and suffering with those of all beings. This makes me think of an “I” no longer confined to an isolated self: the suffering of other beings is no longer something that has nothing to do with me.

This brings to mind Hannah Arendt's discussion of the “banality of evil” in <em>Eichmann in Jerusalem</em>.<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-3" href="#note-3" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 3">[3]</a></sup> Her concern with “thoughtlessness” prompts me to ask a further question through the idea of attachment to the self: how can a narrow definition of “I” become a condition that allows evil to occur? When others are placed outside the boundaries of “I,” their circumstances and suffering can become “nothing to do with me.” And “nothing to do with me” can make us stop asking what our actions will do to others.

But thinking does not necessarily free us from that narrow definition. A person can think meticulously while planning only for themselves, or for the group with which they identify. Both thoughtlessness and thought can thus participate in the making of evil: the former stops questioning the circumstances of those excluded; the latter may find reasons for excluding and harming them. Opposed as they seem, both can rest on the same attachment—only what falls within “I” deserves concern; everything else can be left out of account. In this sense, thought not only fails to break attachment to the self; it may become its instrument.

The meaning of <em>hui</em> in Buddhism also makes me think of this. The <em>Da Cheng Guang Wu Yun Lun</em> (《大乘广五蕴论》) defines it as follows: “What is hui? Its nature is to discern phenomena in relation to that object. It may be guided by sound reasoning, by unsound reasoning, or by neither. ‘That object’ means what is being examined.”<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-4" href="#note-4" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 4">[4]</a></sup> Here <em>hui</em> cannot simply be equated with the wisdom we ordinarily praise. It involves discernment and discrimination concerning an object of attention; it does not necessarily tend toward the good. The distinction between good and evil may therefore depend not only on whether or how a person thinks, but also on the kind of “I” to which that thinking is attached. If that definition remains narrow, even the most precise thinking may fail to bring someone closer to the circumstances of others. This is the ethical significance I see in breaking attachment to the self, and why I want to use it to reconsider “good” and “evil.” By extending concern, I do not mean absorbing everyone else into “I,” making them “part of me.” I mean no longer deciding whether their suffering matters on the basis of whether they belong to “me.”

So I am less concerned with how complex or ingenious a justification someone can offer for their conduct than with whom they include within “I” and whom they cast as other. Even a “good deed” can contain self-interest; thought, too, can serve a narrowly conceived self. Conversely, when another person's circumstances cease to be irrelevant to someone, that person may become more willing to attend to another's suffering, even without an explicit intention to be “altruistic”—and less willing to treat that suffering as a mere cost of achieving their own ends.

Following this line of thought, I also want to reconsider “I” and “other” in relations between genders. When the experience of one gender becomes the universal measure, another gender can easily be treated as a deviation from it, its circumstances and suffering overlooked. This brings to mind Chizuko Ueno's discussion of gender relations in 《厌女》 (<em>Misogyny</em>).<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-5" href="#note-5" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 5">[5]</a></sup> But my question here is not which gender is naturally better able to loosen attachment to the self. It is this: when we oppose the injustices we experience, can we also recognise our own exclusion of others? Narrow definitions of the self are not confined to any one gender; they can attach themselves to nations, groups and identities. But that does not mean that people in different circumstances possess equal power or bear equal responsibility. Nor does opposing oppression or fighting for rights become another form of oppression simply because it involves conflict. Reflection on attachment to the self should help us see others' circumstances while maintaining our own claims—not demand that those suffering injustice give up their struggle.

Yet when I turn from the individual to our life together, I still doubt how practicable it is to break attachment to the self completely. Even for someone willing to examine their own attachments, continually recognising and loosening them in particular situations is not easy. People still need to make a living, maintain their boundaries and confront injustice. Acknowledging another's pain does not make these needs disappear. I therefore find it difficult to treat the demands of individual spiritual practice as a universal solution to social problems. But that does not mean such reflection matters only if attachment to the self is overcome entirely. Perhaps we can begin somewhere more limited: recognising when we exclude others from our concern, and when, in the name of protecting ourselves, we overlook their circumstances.

Perhaps the significance of this reflection lies in coming to understand our connections with others anew. A passage in Pankaj Mishra's <em>An End to Suffering</em> makes me think of this: “…Like Plato, the Buddha also believed that social life forces human beings to bear inescapable social responsibilities. Within such a collective setting, no individual salvation is possible. In fact, in the Buddha's account, liberation involves entering a state free of self-interest; in such a state, one comes to understand that all things are interdependent.” As I understand it, this does not mean that freedom from self-interest is, in the end, merely another form of self-interest. Rather, once “I” is no longer conceived as an existence isolated from others, the boundary between self-interest and concern for others becomes less distinct. Concern for another's circumstances need not be a sacrifice of oneself. It may also loosen one's attachment to an isolated self and allow a different understanding of one's own situation.

So far, I have mainly discussed the ethical significance of breaking attachment to the self. But two questions still need to be asked. First, I am inclined to affirm concern for others' circumstances and to question how a narrow definition of the self can make us disregard their suffering. Yet reading the <em>Vimalakīrti Sūtra</em> on nonduality gives rise to another doubt: if distinguishing good from evil can itself become an attachment, on what grounds can I sustain that affirmation and that questioning? Second, I have been using “I” throughout without explaining what it refers to. If attachment to “I” is precisely what needs to be examined, is “I” a boundary that can keep expanding—or something that cannot, in the first place, be regarded as a fixed entity?

Take the first question. While discussing nonduality, the <em>Vimalakīrti Sūtra</em> also presents differences and criticisms concerning spiritual practice through the words and experiences of its various figures. So, for now, I am unwilling to understand nonduality as the cancellation of all judgment. But that alone does not tell us what makes a judgment appropriate. In its discussion of the aggregate of consciousness, the <em>Da Cheng Guang Wu Yun Lun</em> says: “What is the aggregate of consciousness? Its nature is to discern its object.” This suggests to me that recognising differences is part of how we know things. Yet the ability to distinguish does not guarantee that our judgments are right. The next question may be: can we make a judgment without treating it as the fixed nature of the thing itself?

In coming to know things, we always distinguish between them—nor can we, simply out of caution about making distinctions, call what we feel to be bad or ugly good or beautiful. But acknowledging these feelings does not make them irrevocable judgments. Things change, and human actions take place within particular situations and relationships. Rather than treating good and evil as the fixed nature of a person or thing, I would therefore rather ask: in this situation, what causes harm, and what allows suffering to lessen? Not clinging to a judgment does not mean abandoning judgment. It means acknowledging that a judgment may still need to be reconsidered.

I still cannot, then, offer a clear and complete definition of “good.” What I can recognise is often only a possibility within a particular moment: when another's suffering is no longer excluded from my concern, when the once-solid boundaries of the self begin to loosen, I seem to touch something worth affirming. Such recognition may occur only for an instant, yet I feel that what it points toward is not confined to that instant. I still want to catch these sparks as they flash through the mind and try to describe their contours in language. But I must also be wary of mistaking the contours I have caught for the whole of “good.”

But another question remains unanswered. Whether I am attending to others or examining my own judgments, I seem to be assuming an “I” capable of thinking, acting and bearing the consequences. If “I” is not a fixed and unchanging entity, how are the connections between these activities to be understood?

The <em>Diamond Sūtra</em> says: “The past mind cannot be grasped; the present mind cannot be grasped; the future mind cannot be grasped.”<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-6" href="#note-6" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 6">[6]</a></sup> These words make me ask again: when I call the past, the present and the future alike “I,” what am I assuming remains unchanged? The <em>Da Cheng Guang Wu Yun Lun</em> also lists “attachment to a self as a unity, attachment to a self as experiencer, and attachment to a self as agent.” In light of Yinshun's explanation, I understand attachment to a self as experiencer to mean taking the “I” that undergoes pleasure, pain and karmic consequences to be a fixed entity. What troubles me here is not whether people experience pleasure and pain, but whether undergoing them, acting and bearing the consequences must presuppose an unchanging “I” that persists throughout. This is the question I want to pursue through causality and continuity.

Mishra's account of Yogācāra thought in <em>An End to Suffering</em> offers one way into this question:<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-7" href="#note-7" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Note 7">[7]</a></sup> “The thinkers of the Buddhist Yogācāra school held that once a person acts, the traces of memory produced by that action remain until death. Using language similar to that of Freud, they held that all a person's thoughts, words and deeds leave karmic traces in consciousness. This consciousness contains the lingering memories of past lives, like a bridge connecting a person's many lives, rather than only a single lifetime.” What interests me here is action and the traces it leaves, and how they connect experiences of life across time. Acknowledging such connections does not necessarily mean acknowledging a fixed and unchanging “I”: past actions can continue to affect the present, while the present also changes what may follow.

I therefore try to understand “I” as a river rather than a fixed and unchanging entity. “I” comes from the past and moves toward the future. The selves of different times are connected, but need not always retain the same form. When I think back to myself as a child, what I feel is often not familiarity but strangeness. Beyond a few thin memories, I find it difficult to re-enter that state of mind, to understand thoughts that once felt so real. One day, ten years from now, if I read what I am writing today, perhaps I will feel something similar: it is clearly “I,” yet no longer the “I” of this moment. This strangeness proves nothing. But it makes me wonder whether I have all along mistaken the continuity of “I” for its constancy. A river does not lose all connection with its source merely because it flows. Nor can we take the water of any one moment for the whole river.

And all these selves are connected through time by their various karmic ties. Perhaps from here we can begin to understand: even if “the past mind cannot be grasped; the present mind cannot be grasped; the future mind cannot be grasped,” we can still “arouse the resolve for unsurpassed, complete awakening.” <em>An End to Suffering</em> states: “All things in the world arise through action—karmic causes.” Following the Yogācāra account of ālayavijñāna, or storehouse consciousness, and karmic seeds, I try to understand it this way: past actions leave seeds that bear fruit when the appropriate conditions come together. But these seeds are not sealed away, unmoving; like “I,” they too are continually changing. To me, then, “arousing the resolve for unsurpassed, complete awakening” is something like planting a pure seed. Without assuming an eternal, unchanging “I,” one can still form that resolve here and now, and turn toward the good.

Seen this way, breaking attachment to the self concerns not only “good,” but also the question of who I am. I begin to understand why insight into the Buddha's teachings can scarcely be separated from a clear and unclouded wisdom. Breaking through mistaken views and obscurations, seeing the true nature of things, may also be one way of approaching “good.” In his commentary on the <em>Da Cheng Guang Wu Yun Lun</em>, Yinshun says: “To be liberated from birth and death, one must be free of afflictions; to be free of afflictions, one must realise the nature of phenomena, realise the truth; to realise the truth, one must have wisdom.” The words “With the sword of wisdom, cut through the thieves of affliction” in the <em>Vimalakīrti Sūtra</em> also make me think of this: perhaps what we need to break through is not only certain mistaken views, but also the “I” that makes us cling to them.

I seem to have discussed many things in these pages. Yet looking back, I find that my concern keeps circling around “I” itself—how it is defined, how it flows through time, and how it places others outside itself. Thought can help me see all this; it can also become my reason for evading it. At this point, I still cannot claim to understand “I.” But perhaps this is where the questioning begins.

### Translator's note

This is a translation of a personal essay, not an independent account of Buddhist doctrine. Its interpretations, qualifications and questions follow the author's revised Chinese text. Quotations from Buddhist texts and Yinshun's commentary have been translated from the Chinese passages quoted there; the passages attributed to Pankaj Mishra are provisional back-translations. None should be taken as the exact wording of an existing English edition. Source editions, omissions and page references remain to be checked. “Attachment to the self” renders 我执 throughout; it is not used as a synonym for selfishness, ordinary self-care, or a clinical account of the ego. The romanised <em>hui</em> (慧) preserves the distinction the essay draws between a technical term for discernment and “wisdom” in its ordinary approving sense.
