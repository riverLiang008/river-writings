# AI and the Unequal Starting Point

*Nothing Serious · 001*

This essay is also available on [Medium](https://medium.com/@houliang008/ai-and-the-unequal-starting-point-5d72460ad0ab).

## Abstract

When generative AI became widely used in 2023, I was still an undergraduate student. Since then, AI has not only played a part in the development of my professional skills, but has also gradually become part of my cognitive infrastructure and my way of life. Through my experiences of studying abroad, working, creating things, and recently looking for a job, I have started to think about what AI means to different people. Can it make up for some of the information capital that a person did not receive from their family? Can it change the conditions under which people with different levels of accumulated capital compete professionally? As a young person who has benefited from this technology and may also be negatively affected by it, I would like to explore these questions through my own experiences.

## 00. A Job Application

Recently, I applied for a research position. I was asked to complete two sets of exercises so that the lab could assess whether I was a good fit for its research work. I spent several days working on them. My submission included pseudocode that I wrote myself, as well as code that I implemented with the help of AI. In the end, I was rejected because my answers contained AI-generated content.

In the original email, there was a sentence:

> AI tools are multipliers of existing skills.

I do not disagree with this statement. For a research position, it is reasonable to ask candidates to demonstrate fundamental skills without relying on AI. We had different understandings of the requirements, and I could have clarified them before I started.

However, this experience made me think about another question: what does AI mean to different people?

## 01. A Technological Generation Gap

When generative AI became widely used in 2023, I was in my third year of university. I started doing internships in my final year. After that came my postgraduate studies, more internships, and full-time employment.

In other words, I have had very little academic or professional experience in which AI played no part.

For some people who developed their professional skills and working methods before the arrival of AI, it is a tool that came later. They first learned how to solve problems, and then learned how to use AI to solve them more efficiently.

But my experience was different.

While learning how to solve problems, I was also learning how to solve them with AI. AI has not only helped me make better use of my existing skills, but has also played a part in developing those skills.

This may be a kind of technological generation gap. It is not necessarily about age. It has more to do with the technological environment in which a person develops their way of working.

Of course, being able to complete a task with AI is not the same as being able to complete it independently. These are different abilities, and they can be assessed in different ways.

I admit that my skills often cannot keep up with my ideas. I was not a student who excelled academically in my major, and it has only been a little over a year since I finished my master's degree. I have not had enough time to build deep technical expertise.

As a result, AI has a significant influence on how my abilities are expressed. It allows me to turn some ideas that would otherwise be limited by my skills into actual results. It also allows me to learn new things in the process.

Gradually, this way of working with AI has become my own way of working.

## 02. AI as Cognitive Infrastructure

I later realised that the way I use AI might not be all that common either.

Often, I do not finish thinking about something before giving AI a clearly defined task. I may only have a vague idea, or simply be confused about something. Through discussions with AI, I gradually form my own judgement. Sometimes I even discover that the problem I originally wanted to solve is not the one I am really interested in.

This is no longer limited to my work.

I use AI to discuss the design of software systems, as well as characters in literary works. I work with AI to explore visual design, make videos, look for jobs, and even reflect on the problems I encounter while looking for jobs.

My personal website is one example. From organising information and designing the visual appearance to implementing the website, I went back and forth between my own ideas, feedback from AI, and the actual work. I later made a video about how I designed the website.

[Video: How I Designed My Portfolio](https://youtu.be/ltQGNhfGXwY?si=D_L72sq5KpLSk-gg)

Another example is Odyssey, an AI agent platform prototype that I designed. Much of its value comes from the problems I identified, the concepts I developed, and the way I organised the system. But when it came to implementation, I wrote almost none of the code by hand.

I also documented my design ideas and made videos to explain them. Without AI, it would have been difficult to complete all this work with the time and energy I had available. But even with AI, I spent quite a long time exploring and improving the way we worked together, gradually developing a workflow of my own.

And that is how I ended up with the work I have today. None of it is perfect. Some of it may even be rather naive. But I made it.

Looking back at the work I have done in the month or so since I lost my job, I find a fairly consistent pattern behind these seemingly different activities:

- **Job search:** Exploring opportunities with AI, thinking about my career direction, revising my CV, and adjusting my strategy based on feedback from the job market.
- **Odyssey:** Developing concepts and design principles, testing my reasoning through discussion, and using AI to help with implementation.
- **Creative writing:** Keeping control of my own creative work while using AI for textual analysis, character analysis, and translation.
- **Video production:** Going from an initial idea to scripts, shot planning, asset management, recording, and publishing.
- **Self-reflection:** Developing a new understanding of my experiences, working methods, and values through ongoing conversations.

The way I work with AI seems to have gone beyond what we usually mean by using an AI tool. A common way of using AI is: I have a task, I give it to AI, and I get a result. My approach is closer to **making AI part of my cycle of thinking, decision-making, production, and feedback.**

I do not always accept what AI gives me. I say PASS, but I also say no, rewrite this, or this direction does not make sense. Sometimes, halfway through a discussion, I discover a question that is more worth exploring than the original one.

Perhaps this can be understood through a concept in cognitive science called distributed cognition: thinking does not take place entirely inside an individual's brain. It is distributed across people, tools, documents, and the surrounding environment.

I see AI not only as a tool for getting things done, but also as a kind of **cognitive infrastructure**.

Even the article you are reading now is a result of ongoing discussions between AI and me.

Of course, this does not mean that involving AI is always helpful. It can give me incorrect information or reinforce my existing biases. Being able to question it, reject its suggestions, and test our judgements through real actions remains an important part of this way of working.

I do not know whether this way of life is suitable for other people. But at least so far, I believe it has had a fairly positive influence on how I think and act.

Why is that?

## 03. The Information Capital I Did Not Inherit

In 2023, AI helped me with something quite important to me: applying to study abroad.

I did not spend any money on an education consultant. From learning about universities and application procedures to preparing the application materials, I completed the entire process through my own exploration and collaboration with AI. I was later admitted to a university in Singapore.

At the time, I probably just thought I had found a more affordable solution. But through my later experiences of studying and working, I gradually realised that one important resource my family had not been able to provide me with sufficiently while I was growing up was information.

Having access to information on the internet does not necessarily mean being able to use it effectively. For someone without the relevant background knowledge, search results are often scattered and almost high-entropy. It is difficult to organise them into a meaningful understanding within a short period of time. In real life, the time available to us is often limited: we need to collect information, analyse it, make a decision, and act within that time.

If I only understand the value of an opportunity after it has disappeared, then even if I eventually obtain the right information, much of its value to me at that particular moment has already been lost.

Useful information, therefore, is more than a collection of facts that can be found on the internet.

It includes an understanding of different professions and industries, the ability to recognise opportunities, an understanding of how society works, and knowing whom to ask when faced with an unfamiliar problem. These things are closely related to a family's educational experience, cultural capital, and social connections.

Some people know from a very early age what is worth doing, when to do it, and how to do it. Others may spend a long time exploring before they realise that such options even exist.

Before obtaining certain information, they may not even know that they should be looking for it.

Of course, AI cannot give me real social connections. Nor can it replace the trust, resources, and opportunities that come with human relationships. But it has reduced some of the costs of obtaining information and exploring unfamiliar areas. It has also given me something I can discuss problems with on an ongoing basis.

I think this may be one reason why AI has been so valuable to me.

It has made up for some of the information capital that my family was not able to provide me with sufficiently.

## 04. Competing from Unequal Starting Points

At my previous job, I met a colleague who was around my age.

He was a very capable young engineer. As I got to know him and worked with him, I gradually realised that he had more than professional expertise in a particular field. He also had considerable cultural capital, access to information, and resources that I had rarely encountered before.

These things were certainly the result of his own abilities and efforts, but some of them also required a considerable amount of time, money, access to information, and social connections to acquire.

In other words, behind the abilities a person demonstrates in the workplace, there is often a large infrastructure supporting them.

It would have been almost impossible for me to make up for all of these differences on my own within a short period of time.

Interestingly, although we were responsible for different things at work, the difference in how much say we had in certain professional discussions, and in the responsibilities we took on, was not nearly as noticeable. I also participated in the design and development of product prototypes. Later, I designed an automated testing system on my own and implemented it with the help of AI. I was even assigned an intern to supervise.

These things seemed quite normal at the time, but I always knew that they were not quite so ordinary.

My own abilities certainly played a part. But I also have to acknowledge the role of AI in narrowing the information gap and reducing the costs of learning and engineering implementation.

I did not need to acquire exactly the same knowledge or go through the same experiences as my colleague before I could work effectively with him on certain problems.

Interestingly, I was later laid off, and he was not.

I do not know all the reasons behind this outcome, and I do not think it can be used as a simple measure of the difference between our abilities. But it does remind me that being able to participate in professional competition and achieving the same outcome in that competition are two different things.

AI can reduce some of the costs of acquiring knowledge and skills, but it cannot directly give someone social connections, economic capital, professional reputation, or the power to decide how opportunities are distributed.

Still, I think reducing even some of these costs is already quite significant.

Because it may change the conditions under which people with different levels of accumulated capital compete professionally.

## 05. Beyond the Multiplier

Let us return to the sentence at the beginning of this article: AI tools are multipliers of existing skills.

I think what gets multiplied may be different for different people.

AI can multiply a person's existing professional skills, but it can also amplify their curiosity, willingness to learn, ability to explore, and creativity. It can even play a part in developing these abilities.

And if we look beyond individual abilities to the social environment in which a person lives, the significance of AI may go even further.

For someone who already has extensive knowledge, access to information, and social resources, AI may mean being able to make more efficient use of those resources. For someone who lacks some of them, AI may also mean finally being able to try things that were previously difficult to attempt.

These two changes do not contradict each other. AI may help narrow certain gaps while also widening existing ones.

I do not know whether AI will ultimately make the world more equal or less equal. My own experiences are not enough to answer that question.

But I have also realised that I may be both a beneficiary of this technology and someone negatively affected by it. AI has opened up many new possibilities for me, while the changes it brings to knowledge work may also be one of the reasons why the job market has become more difficult.

In the few years since I entered adult life, AI has not only helped me complete a great deal of work, but has also gradually made me aware of possibilities I did not know existed and given me a chance to explore them.

Overall, though, I am still quite grateful for AI. Looking for a job is only one stage of my life, and the influence of this technology on me goes beyond my career.

The world before AI is gone for good.

AI has changed not only what I can do, but also who I can become. And at least for now, I am a little bit proud of myself.
