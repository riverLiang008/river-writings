# And Then?

## How the Question Found Me

This question entered my life one year after I graduated from
university.

I quit my first full-time job after only six months.

Then I joined the company where I work now.

At first, I thought I was solving the problem.

A better job.

Better pay.

A different environment.

But one day, I realized something unsettling.

I couldn't optimize my life simply by changing jobs.

Not even by finding someone I truly loved.

Yes.

There was a frightening question.

**"And then?"**

What if I got a better job?

What if I had savings?

What if I met the person I loved?

**And then?**

Once I asked that question,

**I couldn't ignore it anymore.**

Because maybe the most frightening possibility isn't:

**Something is wrong with my life.**

Maybe it's this:

**Everything is fine.**

So why does it still feel like

**it's not enough?**

## The Endless Optimization

We're used to thinking about life as something we continuously optimize.

If the job isn't good enough, find a better one.

If the salary isn't high enough, make more money.

If you're lonely, find love.

If life feels boring, travel.

There's nothing wrong with these choices.

Very often, they really do make life better.

But there's a strange question underneath them:

**When does the optimization end?**

We seem to assume that if we solve just one more problem,

eventually life will arrive at some complete state.

A little more successful.

A little richer.

A little freer.

The right person.

And eventually,

everything will finally be right.

But I've started to wonder:

**What if that state doesn't exist?**

Sometimes, the feeling that something is "not enough" isn't really about
one thing being inadequate.

Maybe the job isn't the problem.

Maybe we don't simply need more money.

Maybe our partner doesn't need to love us more.

Maybe we don't need to travel farther.

Maybe the problem is harder to name:

**My life seems to be missing a structure that actually belongs to me.**

When that hunger is difficult to describe,

it's easy to turn it into a concrete problem.

A new job will make me excited again.

More money will make me free.

The right person will make me feel complete.

If I quit and travel, maybe I'll find myself again.

And these things can work.

At least for a while.

A new job is exciting.

New love can make the world feel alive again.

Travel can make ordinary life disappear for a moment.

But eventually,

the job becomes routine.

Love becomes part of everyday life.

The trip ends.

And the question returns.

**And then?**

## Twenty-Five and Fifty

Sometimes I wonder what happens when the same question appears at
twenty-five,

and when it appears at fifty.

At twenty-five,

you may not have much yet.

Not much money.

No senior title.

No house.

Maybe no family of your own.

You may not even know who you're going to become.

That's frightening.

But it also means:

**Not much has hardened around you yet.**

You have less.

But you may still have a lot of room.

You can change jobs, move somewhere new, learn something different, try
something that fails ---

even redefine who you are.

At fifty, things may look completely different.

You may have built a great deal:

a career,

an income,

a home,

a family,

an identity,

decades of relationships and responsibilities.

From the outside,

your life may even look quite good.

And then one day you ask:

**Is this the life I actually wanted?**

Suddenly, the question becomes much heavier.

Not because you have nothing to lose.

Quite the opposite.

**You may have built too much to walk away from easily.**

The problem at twenty-five might be:

**I don't know what I should build.**

The problem at fifty might be:

**I've built all of this --- and suddenly I don't know why I built it.**

It's a strange possibility.

When you're young,

you may have less,

but more room to begin again.

Years later,

you may have more,

while rearranging your life costs much more.

Sometimes what limits our choices isn't having nothing.

It's having so much

that every change requires giving something up.

## Love Isn't the Answer Either

This is also why I think love is beautiful,

but we sometimes ask too much of it.

We ask another person:

Make me feel alive again.

Give me something to look forward to.

Make life feel interesting.

But that's an enormous responsibility to place on another person.

No one can permanently carry the demand:

**Please keep making me feel that being alive is interesting.**

New love can absolutely make us feel intensely alive.

You wait for a message.

You wonder what another person is thinking.

Their existence makes you notice things you wouldn't have noticed
before.

Suddenly, there's another consciousness in the world that you want to
understand.

And that can be beautiful.

But if my own life has no structure of its own,

then one day,

when love becomes ordinary life too,

the question may return.

**And then?**

Maybe I start wondering:

Was it the wrong person?

Then someone new appears.

A new story begins.

Life feels vivid again.

But---

**and then?**

Love can make a life enormously richer.

**But it cannot replace a life.**

## So Is the Answer to Follow Your Dreams?

At this point, an obvious answer seems to appear.

Quit.

Travel.

Do what you actually love.

Follow your dreams.

But maybe it's still not that simple.

Because what if one day---

the dream comes true?

You become a creator.

You make the work you wanted to make.

People recognize it.

Maybe you even make enough money from it.

And then?

If we're always searching for one thing that will finally end the
question,

maybe we're only replacing one answer with another.

Work.

Love.

Travel.

A dream.

When one answer stops working,

we look for the next.

But the question remains.

**And then?**

## Maybe We Don't Need a Final Answer

So I've started to think:

maybe a good life isn't a life where we finally find one thing

that means we never have to ask,

**"And then?"**

Maybe no such thing exists.

Not work.

Not money.

Not marriage.

Not love.

Not travel.

Maybe not even a dream.

Maybe what matters is this:

**Don't let any one thing become your only source of meaning.**

Keep some part of your life

that hasn't already been claimed by work,

family,

a relationship,

or an identity.

Some time.

Some attention.

Some money.

Some curiosity.

Some things you don't yet know what to do with.

Because that space allows new things to happen.

You can change.

You can choose again.

You can abandon an old answer.

And sometimes,

if you move far enough from where you started,

you realize that the questions that once troubled you so deeply

don't need answers anymore.

Because you're no longer the person who first asked them.

Your position has changed.

What you have has changed.

The world you can see has changed.

**The problem wasn't solved.**

**It was transcended---**

**until it was no longer a problem.**

## And Then?

So maybe what we need isn't an answer that can permanently silence the question, "And then?"

Maybe what we need is **a life that can keep growing.**

So when the question returns, we still have enough room to change, to create, and to move somewhere new.

So---**and then?** I don't know. But maybe **not knowing isn't the most frightening thing.**

Maybe the most frightening thing is the day **we stop asking.**
