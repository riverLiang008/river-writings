# The Cost of Being Needed

This essay is also available on [Medium](https://medium.com/@houliang008/the-cost-of-being-needed-47fa3c87e1af).

**Abstract**

Starting from observations of self-exploitation in the workplace, this essay explores the relationship between being needed, self-worth, and individual freedom. It examines three central ideas:

- **The distinction between bargaining power and a stable sense of self.** Having the material conditions to say no does not necessarily mean being psychologically able to live without constantly proving oneself.
- **The spillover effects of self-worth validation.** The way a person confirms their own worth may not only limit their own freedom but also affect their tolerance of how others choose to live.
- **Inequality in the ability to generate meaning.** People differ not only in the resources available to them, but also in their ability to find meaning in the world and generate meaning in their own lives.

Through these ideas, this essay seeks to understand the conditions that make freedom possible, and how dependence on external validation shapes our ability to understand and organize our own lives.

### 01 | The Price of Saying No

I used to work with people who often stayed at the office until late at night. Sometimes, they continued working even when no one explicitly asked them to.

I found this difficult to understand. Why didn't they say no? Why didn't they leave?

But saying no comes at a price. To refuse a demand, a person must first be able to live with the consequences. Savings, family responsibilities, employment opportunities, and the social environment all affect this ability.

Perhaps those who work late into the night are buying a margin of safety with the resources they have the most control over: their time and labor.

We often mistake a freedom supported by material conditions for one that comes entirely from personal courage.

Yet even if we set these practical limits aside, another question remains:

Why are some people still unable to allow themselves to stop, even when they can afford to do so?

### 02 | Can I Afford Not to Prove Myself?

Imagine someone with a good education, professional skills, some savings, and few financial responsibilities toward their family. This person already has a reasonable degree of bargaining power.

Yet they cannot comfortably spend an afternoon doing nothing productive. They struggle to accept a pause in their career. They cannot imagine a version of themselves that no longer excels.

What, then, is limiting their freedom?

From an early age, we live within systems of evaluation. Grades, rankings, degrees, income, job titles. These measures not only affect the resources we can access but also gradually shape how we understand our own worth.

There is nothing inherently wrong with working hard. It allows us to develop skills, accumulate resources, and expand the possibilities of our lives.

But somewhere along the way, we may begin to confuse two things: gaining something of value and confirming that we are valuable.

When a person works hard to earn money, acquire knowledge, or achieve a particular goal, their effort serves a purpose.

But when a person needs to work hard to confirm their own worth, stopping can become a threat in itself.

The first question is: Can I afford the consequences of saying no?

The second is: **Can I afford not to prove myself?**

These questions concern two different forms of freedom.

One comes from bargaining power: even if I refuse certain demands from the world, I can still sustain my life.

The other comes from a relatively stable sense of self: even if one day the world no longer needs me, I will not conclude that I have no value.

Having the former does not necessarily mean having the latter.

And most of us probably find ourselves somewhere between the two.

### 03 | The Cost of Being Needed

Being needed can be a wonderful thing.

It means that our existence can make a difference to others, that our abilities can solve problems, and that we have a connection with the world. It can bring us income, a sense of belonging, close relationships, and opportunities to participate in a shared life.

But what happens when being needed becomes a necessary condition for feeling valuable?

To maintain this sense of worth, a person may feel compelled to remain useful.

They need to keep learning to avoid becoming obsolete, keep working to prove that they are still useful, and keep achieving new things to maintain the recognition they have received in the past.

Even rest may lose its meaning of its own and become merely a way to restore the ability to work.

Under these conditions, the outside world no longer needs to apply pressure constantly. Its standards of evaluation have become part of the demands individuals place on themselves.

A person may monitor themselves when no one is watching, demand more of themselves when no one is asking, and feel guilty about resting when no one is blaming them.

This mechanism may also extend to our relationships with others.

When someone depends on a particular way of living to confirm their own worth, another person living in a completely different way may become a challenge to that understanding.

Someone who is happy without pursuing career advancement, or someone who loves life without having achieved anything remarkable, may cause others to question the logic by which they have long lived.

This discomfort can sometimes take the form of an unspoken demand:

*Please don't live with so much vitality. It makes me feel worthless.*

A person may therefore not only demand that they themselves follow a particular system of evaluation, but also wish that others would follow it.

Perhaps this is a less visible part of the cost of being needed.

What we give up may be more than our time and labor. It may also include the freedom to understand life, to choose how we live, and to allow others to live differently.

### 04 | Losing a Job

Some time ago, I lost my job.

A job, along with the income, identity, and daily structure it provided, disappeared from my life within a short period of time.

I began to consider some very practical questions: how long my savings would last, where my next job might be, and how to spend this period of uncertainty.

At the same time, however, I found that there were still many things in life worth doing. They had not appeared because I lost my job. I simply had less time for them before.

This made me think again about those colleagues who worked late into the night.

If a few things had gone differently over the previous six months, I might not have the choices I have today.

If my income, social relationships, expectations for the future, and understanding of my own worth had been more closely tied to a single job, losing it might have been an entirely different experience. The impact could have gone far beyond financial loss.

It is difficult to tell how much of a person's composure comes from their personality and how much comes from their circumstances. Even what appears to be part of their personality may have been gradually shaped by their past experiences and living conditions.

Nor can I be entirely sure how much of what I do today comes from a genuine interest in things themselves, and how much comes from a need to confirm my own worth.

But this experience has drawn my attention to one question:

When one way of living can no longer continue, does a person have other ways to understand and organize their life?

### 05 | The Inequality of Meaning

Perhaps everyone has to confront a sense of worthlessness in one way or another.

But the means available to us are not the same.

Some people can find meaning in a book, a relationship, a journey, or an unanswered question. Through observation, thought, imagination, and creation, they can bring something new into an otherwise ordinary life.

Even an afternoon with a gentle breeze can be a complete and worthwhile experience in itself.

Others may depend more heavily on the outside world to tell them: you are doing well, you are important, you are needed.

These are not two distinct types of people. We may all draw meaning from these different sources, but the extent to which we depend on them varies.

This difference leads me to another question:

**Can a person become a producer of meaning in their own life?**

Perhaps the deepest inequalities between people are not limited to money, ability, education, or physical appearance.

There is another kind of inequality that is particularly difficult to measure: the ability to find meaning in the world and to generate meaning for oneself.

It is not a gap that can simply be closed through effort.

This ability may grow out of personality, curiosity, early experiences, education, or the process of rebuilding oneself after painful experiences. It may come from books and people encountered by chance, or from whether one has ever had the opportunity to spend time alone.

Over time, these factors interact and grow into something that is difficult to separate into its individual parts.

The development and maintenance of this ability also depend on material conditions.

Someone who is constantly struggling to make a living may have little time or energy to explore other possibilities in life. Someone who has been expected since childhood to earn recognition through achievement may have had few opportunities to develop other ways of understanding their own worth.

The ability to generate meaning is therefore not simply a personal trait. It is also shaped by living conditions, personal experiences, and the social environment.

When a person's sources of meaning become too limited, the institutions, relationships, or systems of evaluation that provide that meaning may gain greater power over them.

For one person, losing a job may mean losing an income. For another, it may also mean losing a social identity, a sense of order in daily life, a sense of self-worth, and an imagined future.

They may lose the same thing, yet experience very different degrees of loss.

Not only is the ability to say no unequally distributed, but so is the ability to bear the experience of no longer being needed.

### 06 | If the World No Longer Needs Me

I do not wish to become someone who no longer needs the world.

I want my work to be read, my abilities to be recognized, and to take part in things that matter.

I need other people. I need relationships. I need a response from the world.

But I hope these things can enrich my life rather than become conditions for it to feel worthwhile.

I once thought that freedom meant having the courage to say no. Later, I realized that freedom also meant being able to live with the consequences.

But perhaps there is another, less visible form of freedom.

Even if one day the world no longer needs me, I will not conclude that I have no value.

Yet this often seems difficult to achieve.
