# “Go to Xinjiang”

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

## I

The idea began with an argument with my parents. Work was wearing me down, and I was at my lowest.

“Isn't that just what life is like? What work is like?” my mother said.

“But I'm miserable!” I said.

My mother fell silent. Then came my father's angry voice: “What exactly do you want to do.”

He paused for a few seconds, then asked again: “What exactly do you want to do.”

I fell silent too.

“Anything else you want to say.”

“No.” I hung up.

“I'm not coming home for Lunar New Year,” I wrote to my mother on WeChat.

## II

“Go to Xinjiang”—at first it was just an idea born of defiance. Later it became a rhythm—job applications, interviews, slowly planning the trip. Meanwhile, what I wanted was becoming clearer too—perhaps I was looking for somewhere far away to wash the dust off me.

On the day the wind from the Tianshan Mountains blows over my whole body, will I come alive again?

Two weeks later, I had an offer for a new job. The travel gear I had ordered on Taobao began to arrive: a quick-drying base layer, winter hiking boots, outdoor trousers, compressed biscuits… I laid everything out, piece by piece, on the floor of the corridor between the living room and the bedroom. Beside it stood my big 55-litre backpack. It had been with me on the 55-kilometre ABC trek in Nepal; now it would come with me across the diagonal of China, from southeast to northwest.

“Be brave and do what you want to do. You'll have fewer regrets that way,” my mother told me. When my father learned that I really wasn't coming home for New Year, he was taken aback. He checked with my mother: “She's really not coming back?” At last he let out a long sigh. “All grown up, earning her own money. Can't tell her what to do anymore.”

## III

On the day I left, I got up at 4:40 a.m. to catch an early train. It was still dark, with a light rain falling. The station entrance was already packed with people: adults, children, and… students. They were probably all going home for New Year. A year ago I had been a student too, but I wasn't one anymore. I peeled a banana for breakfast and tucked the skin into my backpack when I had finished. Then the display changed from “Waiting” to “Boarding,” and I got on the first train of my journey.

## IV

For the first leg, I bought a standing-room-only ticket. Shenzhen to Hengyang. Seven hours.

As soon as I boarded, I found an empty patch of floor between carriages and sat down. The only other time I had taken one of the old green-liveried trains was when I was very small and my father took me back to our hometown. Watching the train begin to move along the tracks, listening to its clattering, I had thought it all so new and exciting.

But now, sitting on a floor that didn't look particularly clean, with another seven hours of it ahead of me, I realised just how different these trains were from high-speed rail. A middle-aged woman's voice drifted down the carriage: “Anyone without a seat want to buy one in the dining car?” She came over. “Miss, would you like a seat in the dining car?” “How much?” I asked. “Breakfast, lunch and afternoon tea included. One hundred and thirty-five yuan altogether.” “That much?” I hesitated. “We've only just left the starting station. It's quiet now, but this place will be packed later. You won't be comfortable sitting here.” Persuaded, I followed her towards the dining car. “That's right. A girl should treat herself better. A hundred yuan buys you some space.”

Of course anyone would treat themselves well if they had enough money, I thought.

“Going home for New Year, or travelling?” Her voice was loud.

I smiled and said nothing. When I didn't answer, she went on: “You look like you're off travelling to me. Lots of young people are doing it these days… what's it called? Hiking, is it?”

She wasn't far off, actually. There was hardly any hiking planned for this trip, but I was a keen hiker.

When we reached the seat, I put down my small bag and was about to stow the big one along the side of the carriage. She came over eagerly. “Let me help you.” But the moment she lifted it, she exclaimed, “What a heavy bag!” Everyone in the carriage turned to look. She quickly added, “And she carries it as if it's nothing!”

I was about to explain—the pack had a suspension system to distribute the weight. Then I thought that would probably be unnecessary, and said nothing.

## V

A young couple sat across from me in the dining car.

At first I had thought they were both girls. But then I noticed how attached the girl by the window seemed to the person beside her, occasionally turning to them with playful affection. After stealing several glances at the other person, I finally realised he was a boy. They both looked very young. Their luggage lay scattered under the seats, a bag here, a bundle there, some of them stained on the outside. The girl had dark skin, pale lips and somewhat untidy hair. Her features were lovely. The boy was fine-featured too.

Several times during the journey, the girl took off her shoes and rested her bare feet on their luggage.

Watching them, I wondered whether they had chosen each other and come to the big city together to make a living. I couldn't tell whether they were going home for New Year or leaving the city for good—they had so much luggage, and some of the bundles looked as though they contained quilts and sheets.

The boy kept his head down on the table the whole time, saying nothing.

The woman selling dining-car seats came over and held out a QR code on her phone to collect our payments one by one. Once the transfers had gone through, she gave each of us a receipt. “Keep these safe. When it gets busy, I won't remember every single person who's paid for a seat.” Then she turned and saw me. “You, though—I think I'll remember you.”

The scenery slipped backwards. The young couple opposite sat in silence.

## VI

A girl got on partway through the journey. She was about thirteen or fourteen, with elaborate press-on nails and a little pink bag. “Big sister, is anyone sitting here?” she asked in a sweet, girlish voice. “No. Go ahead,” I said warmly. She sat down beside me.

Watching her, I felt a little surprised. There was something so real and alive about how at ease she was with strangers. It struck me that I had never really been like that. In private, I could be bold and proud, but facing an unfamiliar world, I had always been reserved and quiet.

She seemed interested in me. We didn't talk much, but she liked calling me “big sister” in that sweet voice. I was a little puzzled. For some reason, I had imagined she would prefer adults who dressed more elegantly, who were more distinctly feminine. As for me: a black knitted hat covering almost all my hair, a black hardshell jacket, black outdoor trousers, high-cut hiking boots. You could tell I was a woman, but there was a sharper edge to me.

And so the girl, the couple and I sat at one table in the dining car, sharing a few brief hours of our lives.

## VII

It was already afternoon when I reached Hengyang. Coming out of the station, I felt lost for a moment.

Where am I? Why am I here? Where am I going?

Warm sunlight fell on the ground, the buildings, the cars and the people passing to and fro. Outside the station, the crowd was thick. People were calling out for passengers for their coaches, bound for county towns of all sizes around Hengyang. They waved their arms and shouted, their faces full of exaggerated expressions. This, too, felt new to me: it was a way of expressing oneself I didn't know at all. Suddenly I was reminded that many people's hometowns were county towns, townships, even villages—places the train never reached. It wasn't that I had never been to such places. But none of them had ever been my hometown. I realised that even their journeys home were unfamiliar to me.

Hengyang didn't feel very big to me, but parks, railway stations, shopping centres, markets… everything was there. I sat on the bus, listening to people chatter in the local Hunan dialect. A young mother boarded with a little girl and sat in front of me. The girl was very pretty, but she couldn't seem to sit still. She was clutching an assortment of little toys; suddenly her grip loosened and they scattered across the floor. Her mother scolded her, angry and helpless, then hurriedly crouched down to pick them up one by one.

The bus trundled on. Stop after stop passed. People got on and off.

At last I reached the station for the high-speed trains. Here, in Hengyang, sunset found me.
