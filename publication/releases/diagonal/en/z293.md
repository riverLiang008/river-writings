# Z293

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

## I

Z293 is one of the old green trains, running from Zhengzhou in Henan to Urumqi in Xinjiang, westward along the Hexi Corridor.

It was nearly one in the morning when I changed trains in Zhengzhou. I hurried aboard and made my way through one darkened carriage after another, looking for my berth. Children were playing noisily in the aisle—a carriage door alone was enough to keep them happily occupied. I found my lower berth in the dark and carefully switched on my phone's torch. First I dragged my enormous backpack under the bed, then took a disposable berth curtain and a set of protective bed linen out of my small bag and made up the little bed. Once everything was in place, I put my valuables—camera, phone, tablet—at the head of the berth, slipped on my eye mask and pulled up the quilt.

The train was moving. As the carriage swayed, I rolled towards the compartment wall. Clack-clack, clack-clack. I fell into a deep sleep. When I woke, I would be somewhere far away, somewhere I had never met.

Far away. How good that sounded.

## II

It was already broad daylight.

I felt around for the phone by my head. The screen showed it was just after ten. Reaching out, I fumbled the window curtain open, and the desolate landscape of the northwest came into view. I had never seen anything like it… My spirits lifted at once.

I eagerly got out of bed, took my cup and toothbrush to the washbasin in the aisle and washed. Outside, one yellow-earth hillside after another slipped backwards. A scattering of passengers occupied the seats by the windows. I had my eye on the window seat beside my berth, so I hurried to finish washing and claim a good spot for the view. Most people on the train were heading home for Lunar New Year. Perhaps they had made this journey many times, or perhaps they came from the northwest and the landscape no longer held their attention. I wondered whether my look of surprise, curiosity and a little excitement seemed strange to them.

The sky was a lake blue tinged with grey; below it, yellow earth stretched beyond sight. Vegetation was sparse. The land and sky were vast, imposing and desolate. Such a land seemed naturally to give rise to a fiercer, more silent kind of fate.

A quiet girl with delicate features sat opposite me, gazing out of the window. I seemed much “noisier”—always pointing my camera at the passing scenery, occasionally eating a sausage or gnawing on a biscuit.

When I wanted to leave my seat to fill a flask with hot water for instant noodles, I quietly asked the girl opposite whether she could keep an eye on my bags. She made an OK sign.

Over the three days and two nights of the train journey, we barely spoke to each other. Most of the time, each of us let the scenery outside find its way inward in her own way. I enjoyed the atmosphere. There was a strange understanding between us.

The train carried on and soon entered a white wilderness. I asked her, “Is that snow?” She looked up, paused for a few seconds, then nodded. I smiled sheepishly, realising what a stupid question it was. It never snows in my hometown. I had hardly ever seen snow.

I hurriedly picked up my camera to film it. A young attendant came along, pushing a snack trolley. “What's there to film here? Wait till we get into Xinjiang—that's the beautiful part! This snow's barely settled.” I moved my eye away from the viewfinder and gave him an embarrassed smile.

The sky stayed grey all day. Each time the train slowed into a station, its brakes hissed. There was sadness in my happiness, but both the happiness and the sadness were clear and bright.

After a tub of instant noodles, I began to feel sleepy. Reluctant as I was to leave the view, I decided to go back to my little bed for a nap.

## III

A loud, clear voice woke me.

“Coming up is a famous scenic area, with the largest grasslands in the world. It's also where the blood-sweating horses come from.”

The mention of beautiful scenery brought me to life again. I poked my head out of the compartment and saw an attendant wearing heavy make-up. When she spotted me, she grew still more enthusiastic about the scenery ahead.

I got out of bed and returned to my earlier seat by the window. The girl opposite wasn't there; perhaps she was still asleep in her berth. The attendant changed tack and began selling things. “Dried prunes, a Xinjiang speciality! Nearly at the last stop now. The boss isn't here, so we're selling them at a discount. Thirty-five yuan a bag before, now two bags for thirty! Free samples for everyone!”

So I was given a prune. I tore open the wrapper and popped it into my mouth. Sweet and tart, delicious.

It was nearly five in the afternoon. The sun had not yet set. I wondered how far away this scenic area was. It would be sunset soon—wouldn't we miss it?

Outside, one stretch of white wilderness followed another. As the light faded, the landscape grew bleaker and more desolate—as though this train were the only living thing between earth and sky, moving on without knowing what lay ahead.

By about eight, it was completely dark. There was nothing to see outside but blackness. All I could do was return to my berth.

At around eleven, an announcement said we had reached Jiuquan. People began to get off, carrying their bags. The girl in the berth opposite had reached her stop too. Now only a pillow and a quilt remained on her bed. For no particular reason, I felt a little bereft. The man in the berth above mine was in his forties or fifties. Perhaps noticing that I was now alone, he came over to talk. “Are you a journalist? I've seen you with your camera all the time.”

The question surprised me a little. “No. Just a hobby,” I said with a smile.

“Going home for New Year?”

“Yes,” I said. It was the easiest answer.

“Working away from home on your own? Any brothers or sisters?”

“I have siblings.”

“That's something, then. It's not safe out there these days. Making a living isn't easy.”

“No. It really isn't,” I said. This time, there was some real feeling in my answer.

I took the opportunity to return to my berth and draw the curtain, ending our conversation. For some reason, his questions had made me especially wary. Nothing happened that night, and I slept well.

Early the next morning, on the eve of the Lunar New Year in 2026, I arrived at Urumqi railway station with my bags.
