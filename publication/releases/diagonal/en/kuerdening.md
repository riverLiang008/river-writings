# Kuerdening

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

## I

The morning we set out for Kuerdening was clear. From the front passenger seat, I had a view of the breathtaking scenery along the way.

Easygoing music played through my headphones, and my body swayed gently to the beat without my noticing. Compared with travelling by train, sitting in the front of a car made me more aware of space. A train moves steadily forward, with beautiful scenes flowing past the window, frame by frame. But here I could feel every rise and dip in the road, every change of direction. The scenery became more vivid too.

Along this road, open plains and rolling mountains set each other off. The plains gave a sense of space; the mountains' intricate contours added fine layers to the view. Better still were the colours. On this journey, sunlight turned the distant, snow-covered mountains a dreamlike pinkish purple. Closer by, the snow had melted from the low hills, exposing muted earth tones. Together with the wide blue sky, it was intoxicating.

Our guide pulled up beside a small restaurant near the scenic area and suggested we have lunch first. I ordered a plate of pilaf. Two women from our group sat opposite me. One wore a red outdoor jacket; the other looked a little younger. They spoke to each other in a dialect I could only half understand. The accent sounded familiar, but I couldn't place it.

The woman in red took a cigarette from her bag, lit it and began to smoke.

At some point during the meal, we fell into conversation. I learned that they were from Chengdu in Sichuan. They asked whether I had come alone. I said yes. “That's impressive,” said the woman in red.

The portions in Xinjiang restaurants all seemed large. They too appeared to have ordered more than they could eat, and nudged a plate towards me, inviting me to share. I gladly accepted.

Kuerdening is a forest of spruce trees. Driving through it felt like entering a fairy tale. Besides the scenery, I loved the cattle and sheep along the road. When a car approached, they would slowly move to either side. If you looked closely enough, each animal had its own expression, its own way of holding itself. One or two stubborn ones would only take the little steps needed to let us pass after a gentle scolding from their owner.

## II

When I got out of the car, my feet were on snow, really on snow. I learned something through my feet: loose snow was soft and gave way beneath them, while snow packed down by footsteps held my weight as steadily as solid ground.

The sunlight reflected so fiercely off the snow that I put on my sunglasses as soon as I got out. Even through them, the light was dazzling.

All the way from Yining to Kuerdening, I had been full of excitement. Yet now that we were here, I felt at a loss. I took a few aimless steps, breathing deeply of the forest air.

The woman in red was asking the guide whether he had a drone. She wanted to try a camera move popular online called “pulling a spring onion from dry ground”—a shot in which the drone rises straight up. He smiled shyly. “I do!” he said, drawing out the last word a little. “But I'm not very good at filming.”

“Oh, come on. What kind of guide are you?” she said breezily. “Let me do it.”

Hearing this, I hurried after them like a little shadow. Before I left, a friend who knew I was going to Xinjiang had made a point of showing me videos of the “spring onion” shot.

“It would be a shame to go all the way to Xinjiang and not get one of these,” my friend had said. For a while I had been very tempted to buy a drone of my own, but decided against it: I would only use it a few times a year.

Was I about to have a “spring onion” video of my own? I followed them, a little excited. The woman in red looked as though she knew what she was doing. She directed us towards a low hill in the distance. Off we went in grand procession, leaving a trail of footprints in the snow.

A, another woman in our group, was the first to take her place at the highest point. The woman in red took the drone from the guide, who rummaged in his backpack for the controller. The drone lifted off from her hands with a buzz. A was already posing. I watched from the side and tossed my backpack into the snow.

The drone made a circuit, then slowly came down.

“Only one bar of battery left,” said the woman in red. “Have you got a spare?”

The guide gave another embarrassed smile. “Yes!”

With a fresh battery, the drone took off again. Once more, it made a circuit and landed.

“Now the controller's nearly dead.” She gave the guide an exasperated look. “Have you got a power bank?”

He shook his head. “What kind of connector does it need?” I asked. “USB-C.”

“I've got one,” I said.

“Honestly, look at you,” she teased the guide. He smiled sheepishly.

The drone rose again. A was ready with her pose. After another circuit, the woman in red said, “This hill's too low. The footage doesn't look very good. Want to come down and have a look?” Then she looked around, apparently unsure what to do.

I knew nothing about drone camera moves and could only go along with their arrangements. The sky was very blue, and the snow lay thick. The snow on the path had been levelled, but there were piles of horse manure. Local villagers stood around the scenic area with their horses, asking passers-by whether they wanted a ride or a photo with a horse. Their Mandarin was halting, but they knew this routine well. Tall spruce trees stood around the snowy clearing.

“Shall we try that hill over there?” said the woman in red.

I had no objection, of course. Off we went in grand procession again.

This time the filming went smoothly. After A's turn, the woman in red directed me to a spot and told me to pose. “Looking cool!” the guide called. I was secretly delighted.

Once the filming was over, there didn't seem to be much I could help with. I crouched to one side, quietly taking in the view. “Want me to take some photos of you?”

I turned. It was B, another member of our group.

“Yes, please.”

“Don't you think it's strange? We're together today, but after today we'll probably never see each other again. You meet so many people in a lifetime,” B said.

Of everything he said, that stayed with me most. I thanked him for taking my photos.

That day we made a group video. We lined up and fell backwards into the snow, one after another—the woman in red, me, B and the guide.

The final frame held four faces in sunglasses, all grinning, teeth showing.
