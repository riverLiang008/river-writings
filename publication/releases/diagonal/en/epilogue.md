# Epilogue

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

After Tianshan, I stayed in Urumqi for one more day. That day, I visited the Xinjiang Museum.

The man I had spent Lunar New Year's Eve with had told me his only regret was not visiting it. I had also seen photos from the museum in the woman in red's WeChat Moments.

With my black backpack, I walked through the museum doors.

I wandered into a gallery at random. It was a porcelain exhibition, not much different from those in other cities' museums.

I opened Xiaohongshu and found a visitor's guide describing the thousand-year-old mummified remains excavated in Xinjiang and displayed here. In the dry conditions, the bodies had been preserved, their skin shrunken, their outlines still clearly discernible. The weave of the fabrics they wore was still distinct too.

It seemed extraordinary to me. Their souls had long since dispersed, yet their bodies had been preserved in this way, found by later generations, then viewed by a stranger like me.

At that moment I thought of Alzheimer's disease—memories gradually breaking apart, as though the soul were being cut into fragments, leaving before the body. A body the soul has left is like an empty house. But the people before me had long been dead, and their bodies were still here.

Among the mummified remains on display was a man whose identity had been established: Zhang Xiong. He had been Grand General of the Left Guard in the kingdom of Gaochang. It was an epitaph that had allowed later generations to identify him.

I remember the description including the words “Grand General of the Left Guard of the illegitimate kingdom of Gaochang.” As I understood it then, the word “illegitimate”—wei (伪)—reflected the perspective from which Gaochang was written about after its fall.

Leaving the museum, I went to a small restaurant for northwestern liangpi, handmade yoghurt and grilled lamb skewers. The warmth inside kept out the cold. The food left me deeply satisfied.

The next day I boarded a flight home. Several thousand metres up, I saw the mountains of the northwest in their entirety for the first time—ridge upon ridge, like dragons lying across the vast earth. Traces left by water on the wilderness spread like a tree reaching towards the sky. Magnificent. Not a soul in sight.

Five hours later, the plane landed back home.

Was I dreaming? Life is a dream, but in mine there had been happiness and peace.
