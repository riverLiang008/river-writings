# Tianshan

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

The morning I set out for Tianshan was two days after my visit to Sayram Lake. The day before, I had slept all the way back from Yining to Urumqi on the train. That evening, in a small place near my hotel, a young woman in Uyghur dress had brought me a steaming bowl of mianfeizi.

I looked out of the window at the grey sky. The bus pulled away from its starting point, then left the city behind. I knew my trip was drawing to a close. This might be the last time on this journey that I travelled through this land, beneath such an immense sky.

Our guide was a pretty young woman, carefully dressed. There weren't many of us on the bus. We each received a map of the scenic area, and she explained its attractions and the activities that cost extra.

I decided I would pay only for admission and the shuttle bus, nothing else. Then I quietly retreated into my own world, trying to make myself as inconspicuous as possible—ideally, no one would bother me.

We got off at the entrance. Three young men from our bus eagerly took out a phone for a group photo beneath the two large characters for “Tianshan.” The guide went to buy our tickets. I wandered aimlessly nearby on my own.

At last, through the ticket gates, we boarded the shuttle bus. I found a window seat in the front row: the best view. Other visitors got on one after another. Then, to my surprise, the guide came straight over and sat beside me.

The bus started moving, and my mood brightened with the weather. Rows of tall, straight trees lined the road. Light danced across the window, bringing the excitement of not knowing where you were going and enjoying it all the same.

“My ex-boyfriend's in the back with his current girlfriend and his parents. What are the chances? On the same bus, too. I don't even dare say anything. I'm supposed to be up front telling everyone about the place.” She had suddenly started talking to me—and about something so personal. For a moment I was at a loss.

“Oh my God. What a coincidence.”

“I know. Of all days, today. Today would have been bad enough, but the same bus? Do you think I should still go up there and talk?”

She was actually leaving the question to me. “Either way, I suppose?…” I faltered.

She seemed unable to decide, sitting there wrestling with it for a long time.

“Are you sure you don't want a cable-car ticket?” she asked, changing the subject.

“No, thanks.”

“You'll be up on the mountain for hours. It'll be boring.” She seemed surprised. But I still said no.

In the end, she never went to the front to tell everyone about the scenic area.

Once we got off the shuttle, we were free to go our own ways.

The air was sharp, the sunlight bright. Snow-covered conifers towered into the sky. I wandered slowly until I came to a little shop. The view was open here, with a few outdoor chairs for visitors to rest on. I ordered a milk tea and sat down.

Tianchi was thick with ice, and people were playing on its surface. The peaks on either side had contours as sharp as knife cuts; the lake lay in the mountains' embrace. I imagined that long ago, before people intervened so roughly, this had perhaps been a world belonging only to birds and beasts, fish and insects.

Sunlight reflected off the ice pierced my sunglasses, dazzling even through the lenses. I closed my eyes in the sun and sat for hours. I didn't want my journey filled with activities designed to excite the senses. I only wanted to sit quietly before the mountains and water, until the landscape took hold of my inner life like growing flowers and grasses.

I took a photo of the view and sent it to the family group chat. Soon my mother sent a photo from their own trip in another city.

I replied with a thumbs-up sticker.

“Have fun!” my mother said.

The sun gradually sank westwards, towards another peak. I got up to walk around.

I began walking around the lake, photographing Tianchi from different angles. But later, sorting through the pictures, I found that the best ones were those I had taken while sitting quietly beside the shop. Perhaps because it was New Year, there were many visitors, people coming and going along the path.

It was nearly time to head back. I crossed the ice towards the shuttle stop. Taking out my phone to browse Xiaohongshu, I came across a personality quiz: Which philosopher are you? I sat down on a chair beside Tianchi and answered the questions. My result was “Laozi.” For a moment, I thought of forwarding it to a friend who also had some interest in philosophy. My finger hovered over the screen. In the end, I didn't send it.

On the way back to the city, I watched the sunlight fade until all that remained was a grey, desolate expanse of snow. With a pang of regret, I thought: perhaps this was the last time I would see a view like this on this journey.
