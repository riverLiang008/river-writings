# “Through Xinjiang by Train”

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

## I

Xinjiang's terrain is often described as three mountain ranges enclosing two basins. The Altai Mountains lie furthest north; the Tianshan Mountains run through the middle and were the main mountain landscape I would see on this trip. Furthest south are the Kunlun Mountains. Between the Altai and the Tianshan lies the Junggar Basin; between the Tianshan and the Kunlun, the Tarim Basin. Hence the distinction between northern and southern Xinjiang.

I only began to look into this after arriving. Before that, all I knew was that Xinjiang was beautiful. An experienced hiker I knew had told me that once you had been to Xinjiang, other scenic places would lose their appeal. It had something of those lines of poetry about it: “After the vast sea, no other water will do; apart from those over Mount Wu, no clouds are clouds.”

So I had long wanted to see Xinjiang. But I thought it would be years before we met—once you start working, long holidays are hard to come by. Instead, our meeting came suddenly. I quit my first job after just six months. It had all the qualities people considered “good”—security, respectability, high pay—but it wasn't right for me. I wanted life to flow like water: gently, at its own pace, calm on the surface, with a deep energy beneath.

On the morning of 17 February, the first day of the Lunar New Year in the Year of the Horse, I took my big backpack to Urumqi railway station and boarded a train for Yining.

A friend asked me whether travelling alone was fun. I said that, for me at least, travelling alone went beyond whether or not it was fun—it was a feeling that seemed to descend from nowhere. In the city, our identities bind us: parents, children, teachers, students, bosses, subordinates, each with our different places in society. But somewhere far away, somewhere entirely unfamiliar, we are ourselves.

Just before setting out for Xinjiang, I had finished Liu Zichao's 《在午夜降临前抵达》, a book of travel writing about Central Europe. One passage reads: “On a long journey, you sometimes lose your sense of time and space. Familiar daily life seems so remote; everything in your WeChat Moments looks like a carefully constructed story. Because you are moving, and night has fallen, you feel yourself outside the country, outside time. At this moment, all you can face is the black hole within yourself.”<sup class="note-ref"><a id="ref-1" href="#note-1" aria-label="Note 1">[1]</a></sup>

That day's experience was something like this for me. The train slowly pulled away; the city slowly receded. A snow-covered wilderness began to open up, and at its far edge the Tianshan Mountains rose from the earth. From a distance: solemn, majestic, sacred.

The mountains I knew in my hometown in Guangxi were often separate peaks, like pyramids. That had always been my image of a mountain—a single outline, close to an ideal triangle. Only now did I feel the weight of the words “mountain range”: an immense, unbroken presence, towering within the great breathing of earth and sky, sun and moon.

After a while, the train entered the mountains, passing through tunnel after tunnel. I could see their shapes and contours up close. I kept my camera in my hands throughout, ready to record each stretch of scenery. I noticed the words “Through Xinjiang by Train” printed on the window, because they kept appearing in my shots.

Up close, the Tianshan no longer seemed so remote and mysterious. Instead, there was a stillness beyond ordinary things. My whole heart felt as though it were soaking in warm water; my thoughts slowed to an even pace. I had imagined that beautiful scenery would give me all sorts of things to write about, that words would come easily. But now I understood—the opposite was true. Nothing came to mind. I simply looked, and looked.

## II

Stepping off the train, I was swept into the crowd. It was the Spring Festival holiday, and people moved slowly forward with bags and bundles. Waiting always makes people restless; the crowd pressed on. But the children were happy, as children always are, delighted by everything new and interesting.

Outside, I turned back and saw the large characters: “Yining Station.” The air was thick with the smell of cattle and sheep. The ground was damp, probably the remains of snow.

I followed the flow of people in search of the bus stop. There I stood with my enormous backpack, surrounded by strangers waiting for the bus too. Most were families, pulling suitcases of all sizes and carrying large bags. We didn't speak. We knew nothing about one another. But we had waited for a bus together.

After settling in at the hotel, I went out to look for food. So much of a journey is spent eating whatever dry provisions you have. When you can get a hot meal, it is a happy thing.

I followed the map on my phone to Yining's well-known Liuxing Street. Though it was New Year's Day, a few shops here and there were open. The air was mild and moist, the temperature around five degrees Celsius. I wandered along the street, feeling at ease.

Yining is known as “Little Jiangnan,” after the region south of the Yangtze. Compared with Urumqi, the air here was much milder and less dry. The Ili Valley is enclosed by mountains on three sides and opens to the west; moisture carried by the westerly winds rises over the mountains and falls as precipitation.

I went into a restaurant serving mianfeizi, one of the highly ranked places on a restaurant-review app. Worried that the local flavours might not suit me, I ordered a small portion. Following the regulars, I added lamb broth, chilli oil and vinegar, then picked up my little bowl and found somewhere to sit. I was curious about these yellow chunks. The first mouthful was unexpectedly good. I could taste something of flour-based food, with what seemed to be a meaty aroma as well. There was michangzi in the bowl too—what looked like rice stuffed into sheep's intestines.

After the small bowl of mianfeizi, I decided to try something else. I went into another shop and ordered salted milk tea and a naan. I love milk tea. I had once drunk Tibetan milk tea in a shop in Kathmandu; its lingering fragrance had stayed with me ever since.

A skin of milk floated on this tea. It looked promising. I tried a spoonful and turned the flavour over in my mouth. Perhaps it wasn't quite for me. Unwilling to give up, I took several more sips, until I finally realised that I really didn't like salted milk tea very much.

Later, back in Urumqi, I had mianfeizi again. But I never tried the salted milk tea again.
