# Lunar New Year's Eve in Urumqi

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

## I

6:40 a.m. The train was approaching the station. I checked the weather on my phone. Minus ten degrees Celsius.

I hurried into my double-layered outdoor trousers, pulled a wool jumper over my quick-drying base layer, then put on my fleece-lined jacket. A knitted hat came last—I had wrapped myself up from head to toe.

Stepping off the train with my bags, I entered a whole new world. My breath turned to white mist, then quickly dispersed. The cold sharpened my senses, yet deep down I felt a faint excitement.

People hurried along the platform.

I was rich in time. My thoughts could take their time too. Slowly, I made my way off the platform, through the station hall and out to a small square in front of the station entrance. White snow covered the flowerbeds. My bare hands quickly lost their warmth, growing numb and stiff.

Dawn comes late out here beyond the old frontier. At around eight, the sky was still grey. In the faint morning light, I walked towards the hotel I had booked, step by step under my heavy bags. Only then did it sink in—I was in a northern city entirely unfamiliar to me.

When I reached the hotel, its sign was unlit, perhaps because it was so early. I thought it was closed and lingered outside for a long time, even considering finding somewhere else. At last I pushed the door open and saw a young woman at reception. Her Mandarin was not very fluent, but her smile was warm and bright.

After taking down my check-in details, she said she would see whether any rooms were available. I was confused. “Are there no rooms left?” I asked.

“Oh, no, that's not what I mean. I'm checking whether there's a room free now, so you can check in early.”

It turned out that all the rooms of the type I had booked were still occupied. “That's all right. I'll leave my bags here, have a look around and come back this afternoon,” I said.

She gave me an apologetic smile. “All right. Go and have a look around, then. Come back this afternoon.”

## II

I waited at the bus stop.

There was no shelter against the wind, no bench to sit on. Only a solitary sign stood in the cold.

Beneath it was someone with a large backpack. After a while, an elderly man came over, apparently waiting for the bus too.

Me, the traveller, the old man and the sign: four vertical lines in the wind.

Every seat on the bus had a little cushion.

I got off near Hongshan Park. The person who had waited at the same stop got off too. Suddenly I wanted to exchange a few words with him.

We entered the almost empty park one behind the other. It was light now, and the whole frontier city was bathed in morning sun. He walked fast and soon pulled a dozen metres ahead. I jogged to catch up. “Are you travelling on your own?”

He turned.

“Yes.”

Without needing to say anything more, we fell into step.

He had already been around Yining and was spending a day in Urumqi before flying home that evening. He told me a story that stayed with me. In Yining, he had joined a group of six for a trip to Sayram Lake. One of them was a young man who had just quit his job. On the morning they were due to set out, the young man decided the weather wasn't good enough. He simply stayed behind at the guesthouse, playing his guitar.

I laughed. “Once you quit, you're rich in time. You get so little leave when you're working. Every year you can only squeeze in a little time to travel.”

He glanced at me and kept walking. “But without a job, you don't have the money to travel.”

“Yes. It's hard,” I said with a smile.

Hongshan Park was quiet that morning. Thick snow covered the woods. There was almost no one but the two of us and a few sparrows here and there.

At the highest point of the park, a viewing platform looked out over the whole city. The bluish morning light fell on building after building. Cars moved through the streets, and the city looked like an enormous toy. In that moment, a great stillness surged within me. I lifted my camera to record the view. He was filming with his Pocket 3 too.

“Are you travelling alone as well?” he asked.

“Yes. It's so hard to find someone you travel well with,” I said.

“It is,” he said. “Who wants to come away with you right over New Year?”

After Hongshan, we went to Shuimogou Park. Walking down the street, both of us in outdoor jackets and trousers and hiking boots, we looked quite different from the locals. Two figures, one big, one small. Looking back now, it seems a little funny.

Perhaps because it was noon by then, Shuimogou Park was much busier than Hongshan. Most people were young parents bringing their children out for some air. As we talked, we discovered that we had both hiked in Nepal. He told me he had passed out from low blood sugar on the ABC trek, collapsing on the steps and giving everyone nearby a fright. When he slowly came round, a crowd was gathered around him. “It was the first time I'd had so many people staring at me,” he said, making an exaggerated face as he told the story. Some kind people tried to lift him up to help him, and he hurriedly shouted, “Just give me a sugar!”

## III

We visited the International Grand Bazaar together. In Uyghur, “bazaar” means “market.” Though it was Lunar New Year's Eve, there were still plenty of vendors and visitors. Everywhere there was naan, salted milk tea, baked buns, pomegranate juice and yoghurt. I bought a tub of yoghurt. “At this temperature, you don't even need a fridge to keep it from going off,” I said with a grin.

By around four in the afternoon, we had been to almost all the sights recommended on Xiaohongshu.

We stopped at a crossroads. “Where next?” he asked.

“I'm a little tired. I think I'll head back to the hotel.”

“All right. Bye.” And just like that, we said goodbye.
