# Afternoon No. 02

> Date: May 2026\
> Location: Shenzhen\
> Conversation partner: B

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

---

She was the second person I had arranged to meet; I'll call her B. We had originally set a date, but at noon that day she messaged to say she was ill, so we postponed until an evening a few days later. I had imagined we would find a quiet bar to talk in. At lunchtime on the day we were meeting, she sent me a message: “Any recommendations for good food?”

Only then did I realise she wanted to have dinner together. We agreed to meet at a barbecue restaurant at seven. It was close to the sea, so I thought we could go for a walk along the shore afterwards.

At 6:45, I left work. The weather was clear, and red sunset clouds showed between the high-rises. I soon found the restaurant. She wasn't there yet, so I sat on a chair nearby.

“White shirt, loose striped trousers,” I messaged her.

“I'm in a yellow-and-white dress, coming down in the lift!” she replied.

Before long, I saw a woman in a white dress at the entrance, apparently looking for someone. But the message had said “yellow and white.” After a few seconds' hesitation, I walked over. The moment our eyes met, we knew we had found each other.

“Are you…?” “Yes!”

She had large eyes and was a little shorter than me.

We began with work. “Are you graduating from a bachelor's or a master's…?” I asked.

“A master's,” she said.

“And are you going on to further study, or starting work?”

“No, no,” she began. “I'm going to work. Product manager at a big tech company.”

“Wow, that's great!” I said, offering a compliment.

“I'm leaving Shenzhen for another city,” she said.

Talking about our undergraduate years, we discovered that the places where we had studied were actually quite close to each other.

“So you're working on the development side of AI products now?” she asked. That was information I had included in my coffee-chat invitation post.

“Yes.”

“So your degree was in computer science…?”

“Yes. Software engineering,” I said.

“Can I ask what your work involves now?”

“Since we started using AI, coding has become much faster. But complex systems still need people to design the architecture. Development now is more about quickly making a version, seeing how it works, then revising it. It involves some product design too.”

“For instance, we look at similar products already on the market. Of course, we also have colleagues specifically responsible for requirements and interface design.”

“Isn't that a bit…?”

“You mean, isn't it just ‘copying’…?”

“Yes, exactly,” she said.

“Not entirely. The product is quite new. Some things haven't really been put into practice yet, so you can't find an exact equivalent in existing products. And some parts of existing products aren't especially well designed. So I read some papers too,” I said. I felt a little sheepish mentioning papers. It wasn't untrue, but research wasn't my main job; counting them up, I had only skimmed two.

“Then how do you know which papers to read?” she asked.

“Ask AI. Ha!” That made us both laugh.

A brief silence.

“Is there anything you really want to do, but feel you might never manage in this lifetime?” she asked.

The question caught me off guard. “I think after trying a few things these past years—studying abroad, travelling alone—I don't actually have that many things I desperately want anymore. Even with travel, I'm happy to go, but if I don't, I don't feel I have to force it.”

“But don't you think travelling is just moving somewhere else to live the same life, and people romanticise it?”

“Wow, that's a sharp question!” I looked at her with a little surprise. Then I took a sip of water and began slowly:

“Well… not for me. I think if you really pay attention when you travel—to the people you meet, the scenery, the food—there are still plenty of different things to experience. I've got quite a lot out of travelling,” I said.

“What would you say you've got out of it?”

“Um…” That stopped me short.

“Hard to put into words?” she added.

“Yes… but I think the feelings from a trip stay in my memory, even after a long time.” Gradually, I began to feel like a schoolchild answering a teacher's questions, trying to guess how many marks each answer would earn. It left me a little tired, and a little frustrated.

“Then what's the trip that's stayed with you most?” I asked her.

She too seemed to pause. “The most memorable was a trip to Japan with friends.” But she didn't seem to have anything more to say about it.

“I'm planning to go to Japan this year too,” I said with a smile.

“Do you think never having been in love would be something to regret?”

At that, I took another slow sip of water. “Well… I think love is a very elevated kind of feeling. To begin with, both people need to be very good people. Everyone has light and shadow in them. Once you get close, you inevitably start looking at each other through a magnifying glass, noticing all sorts of flaws and things you can't bear,” I said. “If two people can see each other clearly and still understand and accept each other, I think they're both unusually good people.”

“So I tend to leave it to chance. Even if you're a decent person yourself, you need a lot of luck to meet someone equally decent. You can't control that,” I went on. “There are lots of relationships we can't control or choose. The simplest example is our parents. We don't get to choose who they are, or what they're like.”

“But I have a friend whose parents are awful. She cut ties with them after she grew up.”

“Yes. But cutting contact doesn't mean the relationship you had is erased. The memories and experiences they shared might still affect her, even if they never speak again.”

“Cutting contact doesn't mean the relationship you had is erased…” she murmured. Then she asked, “But some relationships are really bad.”

“Yes,” I said. “So you still have to ‘manage’ relationships. Where we do have a choice, it's better to keep away from people who are bad for us.”

“Do you think never having been in love would be something to regret?” I asked her in turn.

She paused too, then began to explain her thoughts.

“…So I don't think never having been in love is something to regret,” she concluded.

I don't really remember the details of her “argument” on this point. By the time I asked the question back, I was already losing interest in her answer. I didn't know whether she too was genuinely searching for an answer. By then, though, I had begun to hear these questions as tests of me.

Dinner was nearly over.

We left the barbecue restaurant and said goodbye at the metro entrance. Afterwards, I went to the shore alone to feel the evening breeze.
