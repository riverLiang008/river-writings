# Afternoon No. 03

> Date: May 2026\
> Location: Shenzhen\
> Conversation partner: C

*Written in Chinese by River. Translated into English by ChatGPT.*

---

C and I have met twice. This is about the first time.

We arranged to meet at a Tibetan café tucked away in a little alley. Later, we discovered that we had both spent quite a while trying to find it.

She was already there when I arrived. The café had three floors, and I wasn't sure which one she was on. I was about to message her on Xiaohongshu when, on the second floor, I saw a girl in a little white dress, wearing pearl earrings, her hair tied back. The moment our eyes met, I knew it was her.

After we said hello, I asked, “Shall we sit on this floor, or…?”

“Either's fine… There's tatami seating upstairs.” Having only just met me, she still seemed a little reserved.

“I love tatami! Let's go and have a look,” I said with a smile.

The third floor was one large room with Tibetan rugs on the floor and four low tables, one of them occupied. I glanced back at C, asking with my eyes, then said, “What do you think? Shall we sit here?”

“Sure,” she said.

We sat down and began studying the menu. Highland barley coffee, butter tea, sweet tea…

“Does the butter tea only come hot? I'd quite like to try it,” I said, looking over the menu again.

“I'd quite like to try it too…” C said.

“I'll go downstairs and ask. Wait here!” I said brightly.

---

“The butter tea only comes hot. It solidifies when it's cold,” I said when I came back a moment later. “That's what the owner said,” I added with a smile.

“But I'd still like to try it.”

“Yes, me too.”

That settled it.

“I went on a road trip in western Sichuan once. We stayed in a Tibetan family's house, with décor a bit like this. In the morning they made us breakfast: butter tea and highland barley flatbread. I remember it tasting pretty good.”

“That sounds lovely,” C said. “I'm thinking of travelling too. I was feeling a bit down a while ago, so now I want to go somewhere different, do something different.” She paused. “It's a bit like one stage of my life has ended, and I'm finding a way to start again—I want to do things differently, but I haven't decided where to go yet.”

“Same here,” I said. “I stayed at my first job for six months and nearly ended up depressed. After I found a new job, I had a month free between the two, so I went to Xinjiang on my own.”

“By train. Three days and two nights,” I added.

“How did you find it?” C asked.

“I loved it, haha—travelling alone. It felt so free.”

---

“Are you studying abroad? A master's or a bachelor's?”

“Yes. A master's. I'm taking a break at the moment.”

She began telling me about higher education in France. “There are universities and grandes écoles. Before entering a grande école, you generally spend two years in preparatory classes, then take a very demanding entrance exam. The grandes écoles are more of an ‘elite education’.”

“Of course, there are very good universities too,” she added.

“I think I've heard of the École normale supérieure. Is that a grande école?” I asked.

“Yes, though they're more academically focused,” she replied. Then she spoke about her own view of the system. “I think it's unfair to people with fewer resources. A lot of people don't even know about preparatory classes or grandes écoles, while others can plan their route from very early on.”

Her words surprised me a little.

“It's the same at Chinese universities,” I said. “It's just less visible. University is an open-ended game—internships, research, competitions. A lot of these things need support from your family's resources too,” I said. “Lots of people don't know what to do with their university years. They just muddle through, haha.”

I had once suffered from that same unequal access to information. Having also read Zheng Yajun's *After Exam Success: Why University Graduates Take Different Paths*, I felt this keenly. But I just smiled.

---

“So you're doing an internship in Shenzhen now?” I asked. “How are you finding it?”

“It's all right, I think. It's just that my work seems to be mostly filling in spreadsheets. I don't feel I'm growing very much through it.”

I gave her a brief account of my own working life too. “The overtime at my last job was too much. I couldn't really take it, so I moved to my current one.” I smiled. “I'd rather earn less and have both days of the weekend off. One day off a week is really hard.”

“I couldn't accept a one-day weekend either,” she said.

---

“I was feeling a bit depressed a while ago, and I read a lot of philosophy. Though nothing compared with you, haha,” she said.

“Oh, no, no, no,” I said, a little startled. “I'm only a dabbler myself.”

“Which areas of philosophy are you most interested in?” she asked.

“I'm quite interested in twentieth-century existentialism. And Eastern philosophy, things like Daoism and Buddhism, haha,” I said. “But I've read much less of people like Hegel.” Every time someone asks me this, I break into a nervous sweat. My interests have always been scattered; I read whatever I happen to come across. If an academic philosopher ever asked me, I'd probably have no proper answer and end up talking nonsense.

“Has anything particularly stayed with you from reading philosophy?” C asked.

“Have you heard of the debate on the bridge over the Hao River—the one between Zhuangzi and Huizi?”

“I think so,” she replied. “But you can tell me about it.”

“Haha, all right, let me show off a bit,” I said, grinning.

“I'll tell it from memory. It goes something like this: Zhuangzi and Huizi go out for a walk one day and get into a debate on a bridge over the Hao River. Zhuangzi sees the fish swimming about happily and says, ‘Look how happily the fish are swimming.’ Huizi immediately comes back with, ‘You're not a fish. How do you know what makes fish happy?’” I went on, “Haha, at this point Zhuangzi probably thinks his friend is being really annoying. There he was, happily sharing this feeling of ‘complete freedom in the world’ with the fish. Why does a logician have to butt in?”

C laughed.

“But he's his friend, after all,” I continued. “So there's nothing Zhuangzi can do except try to put up a fight on logical grounds. He says, ‘You're not me. How do you know I don't know what makes fish happy?’ He turns Huizi's own logic against him.”

C seemed to be enjoying the story.

“Then Huizi counters, ‘I'm certainly not you, so I don't know you. But you're certainly not a fish, so you don't know what makes fish happy.’ That's a killer, hahaha.” I was making myself laugh too. “In the end Zhuangzi is backed into a corner and comes up with a bit of sophistry—I've sort of forgotten what he actually says…”

C and I both burst out laughing.

“Exactly. Hahaha,” I said. “Zhuangzi was in the ‘realm of experience’, and then this Huizi fellow drags him straight into the ‘realm of logic’, hahaha. He must have been really annoyed.” I was still smiling.

“This is nice. Usually, talking about things like this can easily come across as pretentious,” C concluded. “But neither of us feels like we're showing off.”

---

C asked if I wanted to exchange writing samples.

I said yes. My coffee-chat invitation post did say, “If you write, we could exchange writing samples.” Haha. I'd walked right into my own trap.

So I began scrolling through my phone for essays I'd written. My mind was racing: “Which one should I show her? Will it seem a bit weird? Oh God—this is actually a little awkward.”

C looked a little embarrassed too. The silence seemed to go on for a while.

Then we looked at each other, as though we'd come to some sort of decision.

“I wrote this one this winter. It's rather gloomy. You can read this first, and when you're done, tell me and I'll find the next one,” C said.

“Mine's an essay—quite long. I wrote it in a library in Singapore. It's on the gloomy side too,” I said.

…

“I really like your poem! I think you've captured exactly what you were feeling ‘in the moment’,” I said. “I feel poetry is a very spontaneous way of expressing yourself—and in that sense, this is really good. I've been in that state myself, so I can feel it.” I ran through every compliment I could think of.

“I really like your essay too! Though I might not be able to find the right words to describe it. But I really like it. Really.” She seemed to be running out of words too.

She found another piece for me. “This one's more cheerful—I wrote it back when I was still a cheerful little girl.”

…

---

It was already dark when we came out of the café. She walked with me to where I'd parked my little electric scooter, and we said goodbye there.
